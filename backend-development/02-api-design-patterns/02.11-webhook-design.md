## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

A webhook reverses the relationship. For every other endpoint in this module you are the server:
clients call you, and you control the code on both sides of the boundary you designed. With webhooks
you are the *client*, calling an HTTP endpoint that a customer wrote, hosted somewhere you have never
seen, with a certificate you did not issue, that may be slow, down, or gone entirely.

That inversion changes the engineering completely. You cannot rely on the receiver being fast, or
correct, or available, or even friendly — a customer can point a webhook at an address inside your own
network and see what comes back. Every property you take for granted when serving requests becomes
something you must defend against when sending them.

The work divides into three questions. **Can the receiver trust this?** (signatures and replay
protection.) **What do you promise about delivery?** (semantics, retries, ordering.) **What happens
when one receiver misbehaves?** (isolation — and this is the one that causes outages, because the
answer is almost always "everyone else suffers" until you deliberately make it otherwise.)

### Technical Explanation

**Signing.** The receiver must verify that a payload came from you and was not modified or replayed.
The standard construction — used by Stripe, GitHub and others, and now formalised by the
`Webhook-Signature` conventions — is an HMAC over a timestamp and the raw body:

```http
POST /hooks/ledgerly HTTP/1.1
Content-Type: application/json
Webhook-Id: msg_01J8Z9K2M4
Webhook-Timestamp: 1756032422
Webhook-Signature: v1,k9Fx3n… v1,7bQa2p…

{"id":"evt_9f2c41","type":"invoice.paid","created_at":"2026-08-24T09:41:02Z","data":{…}}
```

Three properties that each defend against a specific attack:

- **The timestamp is inside the signed material.** Signing only the body lets an attacker who captures
  one request replay it forever. Signing `timestamp.body` and having the receiver reject anything
  older than ~5 minutes bounds the replay window.
- **The signature covers the raw bytes**, not a re-serialised object. Any JSON round-trip changes key
  order or whitespace and breaks verification — a support burden you do not want.
- **Multiple signatures may be present.** Space-separated values let you rotate secrets: sign with both
  the old and new secret during the overlap window, so receivers can switch at their own pace.

**Delivery semantics.** Webhooks are **at-least-once**. You will retry on failure, and some of those
retries will be for requests that actually succeeded but whose response was lost — the same ambiguity
as 02.8, now with you on the sending side. Therefore:

> **Every event carries a stable event id, and receivers must deduplicate on it.** This is a documented
> requirement on the receiver, not a hope.

**Ordering is not guaranteed** and you should say so explicitly. Concurrent delivery, retries and
per-destination backoff all reorder events. Two mitigations: include a monotonically increasing
sequence or a resource version so receivers can discard stale updates, and (optionally) offer
ordered delivery per resource key at the cost of throughput.

**Thin vs fat payloads:**

```text
THIN   {"id":"evt_9f2c41","type":"invoice.paid","resource":"/v1/invoices/inv_9f2c41"}
       receiver calls back to fetch current state
       ✓ no stale data, no PII in transit, tiny payloads, authorisation re-checked on fetch
       ✗ a callback per event — a burst of events becomes a burst of API reads

FAT    {"id":"evt_9f2c41","type":"invoice.paid","data":{ …the whole invoice… }}
       ✓ no callback; works if your API is down; simpler for the receiver
       ✗ payload may be stale by the time it is processed; PII crosses the wire and
         lands in the receiver's logs; larger signatures and bodies
```

**Retry schedule.** Exponential backoff with jitter (15.14), a bounded number of attempts, and a
terminal state:

```text
attempt 1  immediate
attempt 2  +10 s        ⌉
attempt 3  +1 min       │ each with ±20% jitter, so a shared outage does not
attempt 4  +10 min      │ produce synchronised retry waves across all customers
attempt 5  +1 h         │
attempt 6  +6 h         ⌋
attempt 7  +24 h  ──▶ give up: mark undeliverable, alert the customer, DLQ (11.12)
```

⚠️ **Which responses trigger a retry** is a decision you must publish. `5xx` and timeouts: retry.
`429`: retry, honouring `Retry-After`. `4xx` other than `429`: **do not retry** — the receiver has
rejected the payload and will reject it again (01.2, 01.3). Retrying a `400` forever is a
self-inflicted denial of service against your own customer.

### Mental Model

> A webhook is an **outbound RPC to an endpoint that is untrusted, unreliable, and outside your
> control**. Every receiver is potentially slow, hostile, or pointed at your own infrastructure. Design
> the delivery system the way you would design a client for a third-party API you do not trust — with
> timeouts, bulkheads, circuit breakers and a hard cap on how much of your capacity any one destination
> can consume.

## How It Works

The delivery pipeline, with the isolation boundary that the scenario below is about:

```text
  domain event                    outbox (11.15)                  delivery
  ─────────────                   ───────────────                 ────────
  invoice.paid ──┐
                 ├──▶ INSERT event + subscriptions ──▶ per-destination queues
  invoice.sent ──┘    in the SAME transaction as         ┌──────────────────┐
                      the business change                │ dest A  [][][]   │──▶ worker pool A
                      (no event without the fact,        │ dest B  []       │──▶ worker pool B
                       no fact without the event)        │ dest C  [][][][] │──▶ worker pool C
                                                          └──────────────────┘
                                                          ⚠️ ONE SHARED QUEUE HERE
                                                             IS THE OUTAGE BELOW

  per destination:  concurrency cap · circuit breaker (15.15) · backoff state
                    a destination that is failing is skipped, not retried in-line
```

**Why the outbox matters here.** If you enqueue the webhook after committing the invoice, a crash
between the two loses the event permanently — the invoice is paid and the customer's system is never
told. If you enqueue *before* committing, a rollback sends a webhook for something that did not
happen. The only correct answer is writing the event in the same transaction as the business change
and relaying it afterwards (11.15).

**Destination validation** happens when a customer *registers* a URL, not when you deliver to it:

```text
customer registers  https://hooks.acme.example/ledgerly
        │
        ├─ scheme must be https                    (no plaintext delivery — 01.8)
        ├─ resolve the hostname NOW
        ├─ reject if it resolves to a private, loopback, link-local
        │  or cloud-metadata address (169.254.169.254)              ← SSRF (16.6)
        ├─ re-validate at delivery time                             ← DNS rebinding
        └─ deliver through an egress proxy that cannot reach internal networks
```

⚠️ This is the part most webhook implementations miss entirely. A customer who registers
`http://169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/iam/security-credentials/` and receives your delivery
*response* — or even just observes the timing — has made your webhook sender into an SSRF probe
against your own infrastructure.

## Code Example

Signing, on the sending side:

```python
# ledgerly/webhooks/signing.py
import hashlib, hmac, time

SIGNATURE_VERSION = "v1"

def sign(secret: str, webhook_id: str, timestamp: int, body: bytes) -> str:
    """HMAC over id.timestamp.body — the id prevents cross-event substitution,
    the timestamp bounds replay, and the RAW body means the receiver verifies
    exactly the bytes we sent (re-serialising breaks verification)."""
    signed_payload = f"{webhook_id}.{timestamp}.".encode() + body
    digest = hmac.new(secret.encode(), signed_payload, hashlib.sha256).digest()
    return f"{SIGNATURE_VERSION},{digest.hex()}"

def signature_header(secrets: list[str], webhook_id: str, timestamp: int, body: bytes) -> str:
    """Space-separated signatures, one per active secret. During rotation both the
    old and new secret sign every delivery, so receivers switch at their own pace."""
    return " ".join(sign(s, webhook_id, timestamp, body) for s in secrets)
```

Verification, which you publish for receivers — because if you do not give them this code, they will
write it badly:

```python
# published in the Ledgerly docs and shipped in every SDK
import hashlib, hmac, time

TOLERANCE_SECONDS = 300

def verify(secret: str, headers: dict[str, str], raw_body: bytes) -> bool:
    timestamp = int(headers["Webhook-Timestamp"])
    # 1. Reject stale deliveries — this is what stops replay of a captured request.
    if abs(time.time() - timestamp) > TOLERANCE_SECONDS:
        return False

    expected = hmac.new(
        secret.encode(),
        f"{headers['Webhook-Id']}.{timestamp}.".encode() + raw_body,
        hashlib.sha256,
    ).hexdigest()

    # 2. compare_digest, never ==. A byte-by-byte comparison leaks the position of
    #    the first mismatch through timing, which is enough to forge a signature.
    # 3. Any of the space-separated signatures may match (secret rotation).
    return any(
        hmac.compare_digest(expected, candidate.split(",", 1)[1])
        for candidate in headers["Webhook-Signature"].split()
        if candidate.startswith("v1,")
    )
```

The delivery attempt, with the disciplines that make an untrusted destination safe:

```python
# ledgerly/webhooks/delivery.py
import httpx
from datetime import datetime, UTC

# Aggressive by design: a receiver has 5 seconds to say "got it". Anything slower
# is doing work inline that belongs in ITS OWN queue — which we tell them in the docs.
TIMEOUT = httpx.Timeout(connect=3.0, read=5.0, write=5.0, pool=2.0)

async def deliver(client: httpx.AsyncClient, endpoint: Endpoint,
                  event: Event) -> DeliveryOutcome:
    body = event.serialised                       # bytes, serialised ONCE and signed as-is
    timestamp = int(datetime.now(UTC).timestamp())

    await assert_destination_is_public(endpoint.url)   # SSRF re-check at delivery (16.6)

    try:
        response = await client.post(
            endpoint.url, content=body, timeout=TIMEOUT,
            headers={
                "Content-Type": "application/json",
                "Webhook-Id": event.id,
                "Webhook-Timestamp": str(timestamp),
                "Webhook-Signature": signature_header(endpoint.active_secrets,
                                                      event.id, timestamp, body),
                "User-Agent": "Ledgerly-Webhooks/1.0",
            },
            follow_redirects=False,     # a redirect could point anywhere — including inside
        )
    except (httpx.TimeoutException, httpx.TransportError) as exc:
        return DeliveryOutcome.retryable(reason=type(exc).__name__)

    if 200 <= response.status_code < 300:
        return DeliveryOutcome.delivered(response.status_code)
    if response.status_code == 429:
        return DeliveryOutcome.retryable(reason="rate_limited",
                                         after=parse_retry_after(response))
    if 400 <= response.status_code < 500:
        # The receiver rejected it and will reject it again. Retrying a 400 forever
        # is a denial-of-service attack on your own customer.
        return DeliveryOutcome.permanent_failure(response.status_code)
    return DeliveryOutcome.retryable(reason=f"http_{response.status_code}")
```

And the isolation that keeps one destination from consuming the whole pool:

```python
# ledgerly/webhooks/scheduler.py
import asyncio
from collections import defaultdict

MAX_CONCURRENCY_PER_DESTINATION = 4      # a bulkhead (15.17): no destination may take more
GLOBAL_CONCURRENCY = 200

class DeliveryScheduler:
    def __init__(self) -> None:
        self._per_destination: dict[str, asyncio.Semaphore] = defaultdict(
            lambda: asyncio.Semaphore(MAX_CONCURRENCY_PER_DESTINATION)
        )
        self._global = asyncio.Semaphore(GLOBAL_CONCURRENCY)
        self._breakers: dict[str, CircuitBreaker] = defaultdict(CircuitBreaker)

    async def send(self, endpoint: Endpoint, event: Event) -> DeliveryOutcome:
        breaker = self._breakers[endpoint.id]
        if breaker.is_open:
            # Do not even attempt it: a destination that has failed repeatedly is
            # skipped and rescheduled, so its failures cost us nothing (15.15).
            return DeliveryOutcome.deferred(until=breaker.retry_at)

        async with self._global, self._per_destination[endpoint.id]:
            outcome = await deliver(self._client, endpoint, event)
        breaker.record(success=outcome.delivered)
        return outcome
```

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### Delivery queue topology

**A. One shared queue for all destinations**

- *How it works:* every pending delivery in one queue; a worker pool takes the next.
- *Advantages:* trivial; one queue to monitor; perfect fairness when all destinations are healthy.
- *Disadvantages:* **head-of-line blocking** — a slow or failing destination occupies workers while
  everyone else waits; retries for one destination crowd out first attempts for others.
- *Failure mode:* the scenario below.
- *Use when:* a handful of destinations you also operate.
- *Do NOT use when:* destinations are customer-controlled. Which, for webhooks, is always.

**B. Per-destination queues with a global worker pool** (✅ recommended)

- *Advantages:* one destination's backlog cannot delay another's; per-destination concurrency caps,
  circuit breakers and backoff state have a natural home; a destination's depth is directly observable
  and alertable.
- *Disadvantages:* more moving parts; needs fair scheduling across queues; many queues to monitor in
  aggregate.
- *Use when:* customer-controlled destinations. The default.

**C. Per-destination workers (a consumer per destination)**

- *Advantages:* complete isolation; naturally preserves per-destination ordering.
- *Disadvantages:* does not scale to thousands of destinations; idle workers waste resources.
- *Use when:* a small number of high-volume partners with ordering requirements.

### Payload shape

| | Thin (id + link) | Fat (full resource) |
|---|---|---|
| Staleness | None — receiver fetches current state | Possible; include a version so stale updates are discardable |
| PII exposure | Minimal | The payload lands in the receiver's logs and yours |
| Receiver effort | Must call back and authenticate | Works standalone |
| Load on your API | One read per event | None |
| If your API is down | Receiver cannot act | Receiver still gets the data |
| Use when | Sensitive data; frequently-changing resources | Receivers who want simplicity; audit-style events |

**Recommendation:** fat payloads with a `version` field *and* a `resource` link, so simple receivers
use the embedded data and careful ones re-fetch. Keep sensitive fields out of the payload regardless —
a webhook body is a copy of your data in someone else's log retention policy (16.13).

### Retry policy

| | Aggressive (retry fast, many times) | Conservative (long backoff, few attempts) |
|---|---|---|
| Recovery from a brief blip | Fast | Slow — a 30 s outage may cost hours of delay |
| Impact on a struggling receiver | Amplifies their outage | Lets them recover |
| Your queue depth during an outage | Grows quickly | Grows slowly |
| Use when | Receivers are robust and events are time-critical | Receivers are small, or many share infrastructure |

**Recommendation:** the schedule shown earlier — fast first retries to ride out blips, rapidly widening
gaps, ~24 hours total, with jitter and a circuit breaker so a persistently dead destination is not
attempted at all. Publish the schedule; receivers need it to reason about their own recovery.

## Production Scenario

**Ledgerly, Wednesday 04:15.** A monitoring alert: `webhook_delivery_lag_seconds` p99 has risen from
2 seconds to **4,100 seconds**. By 08:00, support has eleven tickets from customers whose accounting
systems have not received invoice events since the early hours. One customer's payment reconciliation
has not run, so their invoices are showing unpaid to their own customers.

### Symptoms

- `webhook_delivery_lag_seconds` p99: **68 minutes**, normally under 3 seconds.
- `webhook_queue_depth`: 214,000, climbing steadily since 03:50. Normal is under 500.
- Delivery *success* rate: **99.1%** — almost everything that is attempted succeeds. Throughput is the
  problem, not correctness.
- `webhook_deliveries_total` rate has **fallen** from 340/s to 22/s. The system is doing less work, not
  more.
- Worker pool utilisation: **100%**, all 200 workers busy.
- No deploy. No infrastructure change. Event production rate is normal.

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert.** Delivery lag. The shape — workers pinned at 100%, throughput *down*, success rate high —
   is characteristic: workers are occupied but not accomplishing much. Something is holding them for a
   long time without failing outright.
2. **Metrics.** Throughput falling while utilisation is maxed means **time per delivery has exploded**.
   340/s → 22/s with 200 workers implies an average occupancy of ~9 seconds per delivery, against a
   normal 0.6 seconds.
3. **Logs.** Grouping in-flight deliveries by destination is decisive:

```text
$ logcli query '{app="webhook-worker"} | json | state="in_flight"' \
    | jq -r .endpoint_id | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -4
   187 ep_3f81c9a4     ← 187 of 200 workers on ONE destination
     6 ep_9b22de10
     4 ep_71ac5502
     3 ep_c1809fbb
```

   One customer's endpoint is occupying 93.5% of the global worker pool.

4. **Trace one of those deliveries.** Each attempt to `ep_3f81c9a4` takes the full **5-second read
   timeout** and then fails. The endpoint accepts the TCP connection and the TLS handshake, then never
   responds — a half-open state that is much worse than a clean refusal, because a refusal fails in
   milliseconds.
5. **Dependency health.** The customer's status page confirms a database outage at their end starting
   03:47. Their webhook receiver accepts connections (the load balancer is up) but hangs (the
   application behind it cannot respond).
6. **Read the scheduler**, and the mechanism is complete: a single shared queue, retries re-enqueued to
   the same queue, no per-destination concurrency limit, and no circuit breaker.

### Root Cause

One destination stopped responding while still accepting connections. Each delivery attempt therefore
consumed a worker for the full 5-second timeout instead of failing fast.

The amplification came from the retry policy feeding the same shared queue. That customer receives a
high event volume (~40/s at month-end). Every event failed, was re-enqueued with backoff, and came
back. Within twenty minutes, their pending deliveries and retries dominated the queue, and because any
worker could pick up any delivery, nearly every worker was sitting in a 5-second timeout against one
dead endpoint.

**Two hundred workers were behaving exactly as designed. The design had no concept of a destination.**
Every other customer's events were behind a first-in-first-out queue full of doomed attempts — textbook
head-of-line blocking, at the application layer rather than the transport layer (01.7).

The 99.1% success rate is what made this hard to see at a glance: nothing was *failing* in a way the
error dashboards noticed. The system was slowly, successfully delivering a trickle while one
destination ate the capacity.

### Fix

*Mitigation (14 minutes):* manually disable the failing endpoint. Queue depth drains from 214,000 to
under 1,000 in eleven minutes; lag returns to normal. The customer's events are retained and delivered
when they recover.

*Permanent fix, in the order deployed:*

1. **Per-destination concurrency cap** (`MAX_CONCURRENCY_PER_DESTINATION = 4`). No destination can
   occupy more than 2% of the pool, regardless of its volume or its health. This alone converts the
   outage into a slowdown for one customer.
2. **A circuit breaker per destination** (15.15). After 5 consecutive failures, the destination opens
   and deliveries are *deferred without being attempted*, so a dead endpoint costs zero worker time.
   Half-open probes every 60 seconds detect recovery.
3. **Per-destination queues** with fair scheduling, so a backlog is structurally incapable of blocking
   another destination's first attempts.
4. **Auto-disable and notify.** After 24 hours of continuous failure the endpoint is disabled, the
   customer is emailed, and events are retained for 7 days for replay through
   `POST /v1/webhook-endpoints/{id}/replay`.
5. **A shorter timeout with a published contract**: receivers must respond within 5 seconds. The docs
   now say plainly — *acknowledge with `2xx` immediately and process asynchronously; do not do work
   before responding.* Most receivers that time out are doing their processing inline.

### Prevention

- **Alert on per-destination worker occupancy**, which is the metric that would have paged at 03:52
  rather than at 04:15:

```yaml
- alert: WebhookDestinationMonopolisingWorkers
  expr: |
    max by (endpoint_id) (webhook_workers_busy) / sum(webhook_workers_total) > 0.15
  for: 3m
  annotations:
    summary: "{{ $labels.endpoint_id }} is using {{ $value | humanizePercentage }} of the pool"
    runbook: "Check the circuit breaker; a hanging receiver ties up a worker per attempt (02.11)."
```

- **A chaos test in CI** using a deliberately hanging receiver — the test that reproduces the incident:

```python
# tests/webhooks/test_slow_destination_isolation.py
@pytest.mark.anyio
async def test_a_hanging_destination_does_not_delay_other_destinations(scheduler, hanging_server):
    slow = await register_endpoint(url=hanging_server.url)      # accepts, never responds
    fast = await register_endpoint(url=echo_server.url)

    await asyncio.gather(*[scheduler.send(slow, make_event()) for _ in range(500)],
                         return_exceptions=True)
    started = time.perf_counter()
    outcome = await scheduler.send(fast, make_event())
    elapsed = time.perf_counter() - started

    assert outcome.delivered
    assert elapsed < 2.0, (
        f"healthy destination waited {elapsed:.1f}s behind a hanging one — "
        "head-of-line blocking in webhook delivery (02.11)"
    )
```

- **Bulkhead every outbound integration**, not just webhooks. The generalised lesson: any pool of
  workers shared across destinations you do not control needs a per-destination cap. The same applies
  to the tax provider (02.2) and to any third-party API (15.17).
- **Alert on throughput falling while utilisation is high.** That combination means time-per-unit
  exploded, and it detects a whole family of incidents — this one, a slow database, a hanging
  dependency — with a single expression.
- **Publish the receiver contract**: respond within 5 seconds, `2xx` to acknowledge, deduplicate on
  `Webhook-Id`, verify the signature, expect out-of-order delivery. Every requirement you do not state
  is one you will debug in a customer's system.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...a receiver hangs without responding? | It consumes a worker per attempt for the full timeout. Per-destination caps and a circuit breaker are the only defences. |
| ...a receiver is down for a day? | Bounded retries with backoff, then auto-disable plus notification, with retained events for replay. |
| ...a receiver returns `400`? | Do not retry. It will reject it again; retrying is an attack on your customer. |
| ...the same event is delivered twice? | Guaranteed to happen. Receivers must deduplicate on `Webhook-Id`; say so in the docs. |
| ...events arrive out of order? | Also guaranteed. Include a version or sequence so receivers can discard stale updates. |
| ...an attacker replays a captured delivery? | The timestamp is signed and the receiver rejects anything older than the tolerance window. |
| ...a secret leaks? | Rotate with two active secrets and an overlap window; sign with both. |
| ...a customer registers `http://169.254.169.254/`? | SSRF against your own metadata service. Validate at registration **and** at delivery; use an egress proxy (16.6). |
| ...a customer's URL redirects to an internal address? | Disable redirect following. A `3xx` is a destination change you did not authorise. |
| ...the business transaction commits but the event is not enqueued? | The customer is never told. Write the event in the same transaction and relay it (11.15). |
| ...event volume increases 10×? | Per-destination caps bound the damage; global capacity and queue depth alerts tell you when to scale. |

## Common Pitfalls

**One shared delivery queue.** The incident above: one broken receiver delays every customer.
*Instead:* per-destination queues, concurrency caps and circuit breakers.

**Retrying `4xx`.** The receiver has rejected the payload and will again. *Instead:* retry `5xx`,
timeouts and `429` only.

**Signing the parsed body instead of the raw bytes.** Any re-serialisation changes the bytes and breaks
verification, producing support tickets you cannot reproduce. *Instead:* sign and send the exact same
byte string.

**Omitting the timestamp from the signature.** A captured request can be replayed forever. *Instead:*
sign `id.timestamp.body` and publish the tolerance window.

**Publishing verification code that uses `==`.** A timing oracle on the signature. *Instead:*
`hmac.compare_digest`, in the snippet you give receivers.

**Not validating destination URLs.** Your webhook sender becomes an SSRF probe into your own network
(16.6). *Instead:* HTTPS only, resolve and reject private ranges at registration and at delivery,
disable redirects, and egress through a restricted proxy.

**Enqueuing the webhook outside the business transaction.** Either an event for something that did not
happen, or a fact nobody was told about. *Instead:* the outbox pattern (11.15).

**No dead-end state.** Retrying forever fills the queue with garbage and never tells the customer.
*Instead:* bounded attempts, auto-disable, notify, and offer replay.

**Assuming receivers are competent.** They will not verify signatures, will process inline, and will
not deduplicate — unless you publish the requirements and ship verification code in your SDKs.

## How to Test This

- The isolation chaos test above, with a deliberately hanging receiver — the single most valuable test
  in this notebook.
- A signature test vector suite (known secret, id, timestamp, body → known signature) published
  alongside the docs, so receivers can self-test.
- A replay test asserting that a delivery with a timestamp outside the tolerance is rejected by the
  published verifier.
- An SSRF test asserting that registration rejects `http://`, `localhost`, `10.0.0.0/8`, `169.254.169.254`
  and a hostname that resolves to a private address.
- A retry-classification test: `500` → retried, `429` → retried honouring `Retry-After`, `400` → not
  retried.

## Interview Questions

   Forgery and tampering; the timestamp bounds replay.
2. *Explain.* Why sign the raw bytes rather than the parsed object? — Re-serialisation changes the byte
   string and verification fails.
3. *Debug this.* Delivery lag is 68 minutes, workers are 100% busy, throughput has *fallen*, and 99% of
   attempts succeed. — One destination hanging; group in-flight deliveries by endpoint.
4. *Design.* Design retry behaviour for webhooks. Which statuses retry, and when do you stop? —
   `5xx`/timeout/`429`; exponential backoff with jitter; bounded attempts; auto-disable and replay.
5. *Explain.* Why are webhooks at-least-once, and what does that require of receivers? — Lost responses
   are indistinguishable from failures; receivers must deduplicate on the event id.
6. *Design.* A customer registers a webhook URL. What do you validate and when? — HTTPS, resolve and
   reject private/link-local ranges, re-validate at delivery, no redirects, egress proxy.
7. *Explain.* Why must the event be written in the same transaction as the business change? — Otherwise
   you either lose the notification or send one for something that rolled back.

## Key Takeaways

- With webhooks **you are the client**, calling an endpoint that is untrusted, unreliable and outside
  your control. Design for a hostile, slow receiver.
- **Isolate destinations.** Per-destination queues, concurrency caps and circuit breakers. A shared
  pool means one broken receiver becomes everyone's outage — and it will look like a *throughput*
  problem, not an error problem.
- Sign `id.timestamp.raw_body` with HMAC, support multiple active signatures for rotation, and publish
  verification code using `compare_digest`.
- Delivery is **at-least-once** and **unordered**. Say so, require deduplication on the event id, and
  include a version so receivers can discard stale updates.
- Retry `5xx`, timeouts and `429`. Never retry other `4xx` — that is an attack on your customer.
- Customer-supplied URLs are an **SSRF surface**. Validate at registration and at delivery, refuse
  redirects, and egress through a restricted proxy.
- Write the event in the same transaction as the business change, then relay it (outbox). Anything else
  loses events or invents them.
- Alert on **per-destination share of the worker pool**, and on throughput falling while utilisation is
  high.

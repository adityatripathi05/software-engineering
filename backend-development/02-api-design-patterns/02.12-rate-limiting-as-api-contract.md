# 02.12 Rate Limiting as an API Contract

> **Prerequisites:** 01.3 (`429`, `Retry-After`), 01.10 (identifying the client), 02.9 (problem details)
> **What you'll learn:**
> - Design the **dimension** of a limit — per key, per tenant, per endpoint, per resource — and understand who suffers when you choose wrongly
> - Publish limits so clients can pace themselves *before* being refused, using the `RateLimit-*` header fields
> - Separate quota classes so automated traffic cannot starve interactive traffic
> - Distinguish a rate limit (a contract) from load shedding (a survival response)
> **Level:** Intermediate

---

## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

A rate limit is a promise, not a punishment. It says: *"you may use this much of our capacity,
reliably, and we will tell you where you stand."* Everything a client can do with that promise —
pacing itself, batching, backing off gracefully — depends on you communicating it clearly.

Most rate-limiting discussion is about algorithms: token bucket, sliding window, leaky bucket. Those
matter, and they are 10.13's subject. This notebook is about the decision that comes *first* and is
much harder to change afterwards: **what the limit is measured against**.

Choose "per API key" and one customer running eight parallel workers gets eight times the capacity of
a careful one. Choose "per tenant" and a runaway script can lock out that company's human users.
Choose "per endpoint" and an expensive query counts the same as a trivial one. Every dimension
distributes pain differently, and you are choosing who gets hurt when something goes wrong.

### Technical Explanation

**The limit dimensions**, and what each protects:

| Dimension | Protects against | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| Per **IP** | Anonymous abuse | NAT shares one IP among thousands of users; IPs are spoofable behind a bad proxy config (01.10) |
| Per **API key** | One credential monopolising capacity | A tenant with 10 keys gets 10× capacity |
| Per **tenant** | One customer monopolising capacity | All of that customer's traffic shares one bucket — including their humans |
| Per **endpoint** | One expensive route saturating a resource | A client spreads load across routes and evades it |
| Per **resource** | Hot-spotting one row or object | High cardinality; needs care in the store (10.11) |
| **Cost-weighted** | Cheap and expensive calls counted differently | You must be able to price a request before running it (02.2) |

Real systems compose several. Ledgerly applies a per-tenant quota *and* a per-key burst limit *and* a
per-resource limit on side-effect endpoints (`POST /invoices/{id}/send`, capped at 5 per invoice per
hour — 01.2's lesson).

**Communicating the limit.** The IETF `RateLimit` header fields let a client pace itself before it is
refused, which is the difference between a well-behaved integration and a retry storm:

```http
HTTP/1.1 200 OK
RateLimit-Limit: 1000
RateLimit-Remaining: 863
RateLimit-Reset: 47                ← seconds until the window resets
RateLimit-Policy: 1000;w=3600      ← 1000 requests per 3600-second window
```

and on refusal:

```http
HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 47
RateLimit-Limit: 1000
RateLimit-Remaining: 0
RateLimit-Reset: 47
Content-Type: application/problem+json

{"type":"https://api.ledgerly.com/problems/rate-limited","title":"Too many requests",
 "status":429,"detail":"Tenant quota of 1000 requests/hour exhausted.",
 "scope":"tenant","retry_after":47,"request_id":"req_01J8Z9K2M4"}
```

⚠️ `Retry-After` is mandatory on `429` — 01.3's incident was exactly its absence. The `RateLimit-*`
fields are the *proactive* half: they let a client slow down at 90% rather than discovering the wall.

> **Version note:** the current IETF httpapi draft (draft-ietf-httpapi-ratelimit-headers)
> consolidates limit, remaining and reset into a single structured `RateLimit` field, with
> `RateLimit-Policy` surviving alongside it. The split `RateLimit-Limit` / `-Remaining` / `-Reset`
> trio shown here matches the earlier draft and remains what most providers actually ship in 2026 —
> this notebook teaches the deployed convention and names the draft's direction so you recognise
> both on the wire.

**`429` vs `503`** is a distinction worth keeping crisp:

- **`429 Too Many Requests`** — *you* have exceeded *your* allocation. The system is healthy. This is
  the contract working.
- **`503 Service Unavailable`** — *the system* is overloaded and shedding load regardless of whose
  request it is (15.17). The client did nothing wrong.

A client can reasonably respond differently: a `429` means "slow down permanently"; a `503` means "the
provider is having trouble, retry with backoff". Returning `429` when you are actually overloaded
blames the customer for your capacity problem, and vice versa.

### Mental Model

> A rate limit answers two questions: **how much of my capacity may you have**, and **who is "you"**.
> The second is the design decision. Whoever shares a bucket shares a fate — so the dimension you pick
> decides which of your users are hurt by another's mistake. Draw the buckets around groups that can
> reasonably be held jointly responsible.

## How It Works

The failure that dimension choice creates, drawn:

```text
❌ ONE BUCKET PER TENANT

  tenant t_44f1  ────────────── 1000 req/hour ──────────────┐
      │                                                      │
      ├── nightly sync integration  ──▶ 997 requests ────────┤  bucket
      ├── dashboard user (Anna)     ──▶ needs 3 ─────────────┤  exhausted
      ├── dashboard user (Ben)      ──▶ 429  ⚠️              │  at 00:12
      └── mobile app (Chen)         ──▶ 429  ⚠️              │
                                                             ┘
  One automated client consumed the shared allocation. Every HUMAN at that
  company is locked out of the product for the rest of the hour.

✅ QUOTA CLASSES — the bulkhead applied to capacity

  tenant t_44f1
      ├── class: automation   (api keys)      600 req/hour  ──▶ 429 when exhausted
      ├── class: interactive  (user sessions) 300 req/hour  ──▶ reserved; unaffected
      └── class: critical     (auth, health)  100 req/hour  ──▶ always available

  The integration exhausts its own class. Anna, Ben and Chen keep working.
```

This is 15.17's bulkhead idea applied to a quota rather than to a thread pool: **partition the
resource so that exhausting one partition cannot starve another**. The partitions should follow the
blast radius you are willing to accept.

**Where the limit is enforced** matters as much as its value:

```text
edge / proxy (01.10)     cheap refusal; protects the app entirely; coarse dimensions only
      │                  ← put the crude "one key doing 10× its limit" rule HERE
      ▼
application middleware   knows the tenant, the key class, the route cost
      │                  ← the contract lives here
      ▼
per-resource checks      "this invoice may be sent 5 times per hour" (business rules)
```

01.3's incident showed why the top layer matters: a `429` served by the application still costs a TLS
handshake, a Redis lookup and an event-loop slot. Under a real storm, refusals must be cheap.

## Code Example

The policy — declarative, so limits are reviewable and publishable rather than scattered:

```python
# ledgerly/ratelimit/policy.py
from dataclasses import dataclass
from enum import StrEnum

class QuotaClass(StrEnum):
    CRITICAL = "critical"        # auth, health — never starved
    INTERACTIVE = "interactive"  # dashboard and mobile sessions
    AUTOMATION = "automation"    # server-to-server API keys

@dataclass(frozen=True, slots=True)
class Limit:
    requests: int
    window_seconds: int
    scope: str                   # what the bucket is keyed on — appears in the 429 body

    @property
    def policy_header(self) -> str:
        return f"{self.requests};w={self.window_seconds}"

# Per-class tenant quotas. The classes are the bulkhead: exhausting one cannot
# starve another, so an automated client can never lock out human users.
TENANT_QUOTAS: dict[QuotaClass, Limit] = {
    QuotaClass.CRITICAL:    Limit(200,  60,   "tenant:critical"),
    QuotaClass.INTERACTIVE: Limit(300,  60,   "tenant:interactive"),
    QuotaClass.AUTOMATION:  Limit(1000, 3600, "tenant:automation"),
}

# Per-key burst on top of the tenant quota: stops one key of many from taking
# the whole class allocation in the first ten seconds of a window.
KEY_BURST = Limit(60, 60, "api_key")

# Cost-weighted routes. An export is not one request's worth of work (02.2).
ROUTE_COST: dict[str, int] = {
    "POST /v1/invoice-exports": 50,
    "POST /v1/invoices/batch": 10,
    "GET /v1/invoices": 1,
}
```

Applying it, with headers on **every** response — not only refusals:

```python
# ledgerly/middleware/rate_limit.py
from starlette.types import ASGIApp, Message, Receive, Scope, Send

from ledgerly.ratelimit.policy import TENANT_QUOTAS, KEY_BURST, ROUTE_COST
from ledgerly.errors import catalogue as problems

class RateLimitMiddleware:
    """Emits RateLimit-* on every response so clients pace themselves BEFORE
    hitting the wall. Reactive-only limiting (headers on 429 alone) guarantees
    every client discovers its limit by being refused."""

    def __init__(self, app: ASGIApp, limiter: Limiter) -> None:
        self.app, self.limiter = app, limiter

    async def __call__(self, scope: Scope, receive: Receive, send: Send) -> None:
        if scope["type"] != "http":
            await self.app(scope, receive, send)
            return

        context = resolve_quota_context(scope)        # tenant, key, class, route
        cost = ROUTE_COST.get(context.route_key, 1)
        verdict = await self.limiter.consume(context, cost=cost)

        if not verdict.allowed:
            await send_problem(send, problems.RATE_LIMITED, verdict)
            return

        async def send_with_headers(message: Message) -> None:
            if message["type"] == "http.response.start":
                headers = message.setdefault("headers", [])
                limit = TENANT_QUOTAS[context.quota_class]
                headers += [
                    (b"ratelimit-limit", str(limit.requests).encode()),
                    (b"ratelimit-remaining", str(verdict.remaining).encode()),
                    (b"ratelimit-reset", str(verdict.reset_seconds).encode()),
                    (b"ratelimit-policy", limit.policy_header.encode()),
                ]
            await send(message)

        await self.app(scope, receive, send_with_headers)
```

The refusal, carrying everything a client needs to recover without reading documentation:

```python
# ledgerly/middleware/rate_limit.py (continued)
async def send_problem(send, problem, verdict) -> None:
    body = json.dumps({
        "type": problem.uri, "title": problem.title, "status": 429,
        "detail": f"Quota for scope {verdict.scope!r} exhausted.",
        "scope": verdict.scope,                  # WHICH bucket — tenant, key or resource
        "retry_after": verdict.reset_seconds,
        "request_id": current_request_id(),
    }).encode()
    await send({"type": "http.response.start", "status": 429, "headers": [
        (b"content-type", b"application/problem+json"),
        (b"retry-after", str(verdict.reset_seconds).encode()),   # ⚠️ mandatory (01.3)
        (b"ratelimit-limit", str(verdict.limit).encode()),
        (b"ratelimit-remaining", b"0"),
        (b"ratelimit-reset", str(verdict.reset_seconds).encode()),
    ]})
    await send({"type": "http.response.body", "body": body})
```

⚠️ The `scope` extension member is what makes a `429` actionable. "You are rate limited" leaves a
customer guessing whether to reduce concurrency, spread load across keys, or upgrade their plan.
`"scope": "tenant:automation"` tells them exactly which dial to turn.

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### Quota dimension

**A. Single limit per API key**

- *Advantages:* simple to explain, implement and document; the key is already authenticated, so no
  identification problem (01.10).
- *Disadvantages:* a tenant with many keys multiplies its allocation; one key's traffic classes are
  indistinguishable — a background sync and a user click count the same.
- *Failure mode:* a customer issues ten keys and legitimately consumes ten times what you planned for.
- *Use when:* a simple developer-facing API where one key ≈ one workload.

**B. Per-tenant quota**

- *Advantages:* your capacity planning is per customer, which matches how you sell; keys cannot be
  multiplied to gain capacity.
- *Disadvantages:* all of that customer's traffic shares one fate.
- *Failure mode:* the incident below — automation starves humans.
- *Use when:* combined with classes. Never alone.

**C. Per-tenant quota classes** (✅ recommended)

- *Advantages:* a bulkhead in the quota dimension; interactive traffic is protected from automation by
  construction; each class can be priced and scaled separately.
- *Disadvantages:* you must classify every request; the classification must be unspoofable (derived
  from the credential type, never from a client-supplied header).
- *Use when:* the same tenant has both human and automated traffic — which is almost every B2B product.

**D. Cost-weighted quota**

- *Advantages:* an expensive export and a cheap read consume proportionally; the only workable model
  for interfaces where clients control cost (02.2, 02.6).
- *Disadvantages:* you must be able to price a request before executing it; clients find non-uniform
  accounting harder to reason about.
- *Use when:* endpoints differ in cost by more than ~10×.

### What to do at the limit

| | Reject (`429`) | Queue / throttle | Degrade |
|---|---|---|---|
| How it works | Refuse immediately | Delay the request until capacity exists | Serve a cheaper answer (cached, smaller page) |
| Client experience | Clear, actionable | Latency without explanation | Success with less data |
| Resource risk | None — refusals are cheap | Queued requests hold connections and memory | Depends |
| Failure mode | Retry storms if `Retry-After` is missing (01.3) | Queue grows unbounded; clients time out anyway | Clients may not notice the degradation |
| Use when | The default, always | Never as the primary strategy for HTTP | Read paths with a cacheable fallback (15.16) |

**Recommendation:** reject with a complete `429`. Queueing at the HTTP layer converts a clear refusal
into an unexplained timeout and consumes exactly the resources you were trying to protect.

### Rate limit vs load shedding

They look similar and are answers to different questions. A **rate limit** is a contract, computed per
client, enforced whether or not the system is busy — a client at 90% of quota is refused nothing even
during a quiet hour. **Load shedding** (15.17) is a survival response, triggered by system health,
that drops requests regardless of whose they are.

Both are needed. Rate limits give clients a predictable allocation; shedding keeps you alive when
aggregate demand exceeds capacity anyway. Emit `429` for the first and `503` for the second, so
clients can tell the difference.

## Production Scenario

**Ledgerly, Monday 00:12.** An enterprise customer's account manager escalates: *"Nobody at
Nordwind can log in to the dashboard. They say everything returns an error. Their finance team is
doing month-end close and this is blocking twelve people."*

### Symptoms

- Every dashboard request from that tenant returns `429`. Login, invoice list, PDF view — everything.
- The mobile app is affected too.
- **The API is completely healthy.** Global request rate is normal, latency normal, database quiet,
  no errors anywhere. `http_requests_total{status="429"}` is elevated for exactly one tenant.
- Ledgerly's own status page is green, and correctly so.
- The customer's *integration* — a nightly ledger sync — is running normally and reports no problems.

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert.** None. A `429` is a designed response, so it is counted, not alerted on. The escalation
   arrived through the account team.
2. **Metrics.** Segmenting `429`s by tenant isolates it in seconds: one tenant, all routes.
   Segmenting *that tenant's* requests by credential type is the decisive cut:

```text
tenant t_44f1, 00:00–00:12
  api key  key_live_2b8e…  (nightly sync)     998 requests   ← consumed the quota
  session  (dashboard users)                   14 requests   ← 11 of them 429
  session  (mobile app)                         6 requests   ← all 429
```

3. **Logs.** The rate-limit decision log shows every refusal keyed on `tenant:t_44f1` with
   `remaining: 0` and `limit: 1000`:

```json
{"event":"rate_limit_rejected","scope":"tenant:t_44f1","limit":1000,"remaining":0,
 "reset_in":2847,"principal":"session:usr_anna","route":"GET /v1/invoices"}
```

   The `principal` is a human user; the bucket that refused them was filled by an API key.

4. **Trace.** Nothing to find — the requests never reach the application; the limiter refuses them at
   the middleware.
5. **Root cause is a design decision**, visible in one line of the policy module:

```python
# as it was
TENANT_QUOTA = Limit(requests=1000, window_seconds=3600, scope="tenant")
```

6. **Why now?** The customer had enabled a new feature that increased their sync volume from ~300 to
   ~1,000 requests per hour. The sync ran at midnight, exhausted the hourly tenant quota in twelve
   minutes, and the finance team started work at 00:15 for month-end close — the one night of the
   month when humans and automation overlap.

### Root Cause

One quota bucket per tenant, shared by every kind of traffic. Automated traffic is bursty, high volume
and tolerant of delay; interactive traffic is low volume and completely intolerant of refusal. Putting
them in one bucket guarantees that the tolerant workload will occasionally starve the intolerant one.

Two aggravating factors:

- **A one-hour window with no smoothing.** The sync consumed the entire hour's allocation in twelve
  minutes, then everyone waited 48 minutes. A shorter window, or a token bucket that refills
  continuously, would have limited the outage to seconds at a time (10.13).
- **No pacing information for the client.** The sync had no idea it was consuming a shared budget,
  because `RateLimit-*` headers were emitted only on `429` responses. It could not have behaved better
  even if its author had wanted to.

The rate limiter worked exactly as designed. The design put twelve humans and a batch job in the same
bucket and gave the batch job a head start.

### Fix

*Mitigation (8 minutes):* raise that tenant's quota temporarily, unblocking the finance team. A quota
override per tenant already existed for enterprise plans, which is why this was fast — worth having
before you need it.

*Permanent fix:*

1. **Quota classes** as shown above: `critical`, `interactive`, `automation`, derived from the
   credential type (session cookie vs API key — 01.5, 08.11) and therefore unspoofable. Automation can
   no longer consume interactive capacity at all.
2. **A token bucket with continuous refill** instead of a fixed hourly window (10.13). A client that
   exhausts its bucket recovers steadily rather than waiting for a window boundary, and bursts are
   bounded without a cliff.
3. **`RateLimit-*` headers on every response**, so clients can self-pace. Ledgerly's SDKs now slow down
   automatically below 20% remaining — a change that reduced `429`s across all customers by 74%.
4. **The `scope` extension member** in the `429` body, so a customer can see which bucket refused them.
5. **A documented, purchasable quota model** in the API reference: each class, its limit per plan, the
   window, and how to request more. A limit nobody can look up is not a contract.

### Prevention

- **Alert on any tenant sustaining `429`s on interactive traffic**, which is the signal that matters —
  a `429` to a batch job is the system working; a `429` to a human is an outage for them:

```yaml
- alert: InteractiveTrafficRateLimited
  expr: |
    sum by (tenant_id) (rate(rate_limit_rejected_total{quota_class="interactive"}[5m])) > 0.1
  for: 5m
  annotations:
    summary: "Tenant {{ $labels.tenant_id }} — human users are being rate limited"
    runbook: "Interactive quota should rarely bind. Check for misclassified automation (02.12)."
```

- **A test asserting class isolation** — the property the fix exists to guarantee:

```python
# tests/ratelimit/test_quota_class_isolation.py
@pytest.mark.anyio
async def test_automation_exhaustion_does_not_block_interactive(client, tenant, api_key, session):
    for _ in range(AUTOMATION_LIMIT + 5):
        await client.get("/v1/invoices", headers=api_key)      # burn the automation class

    assert (await client.get("/v1/invoices", headers=api_key)).status_code == 429
    interactive = await client.get("/v1/invoices", headers=session)
    assert interactive.status_code == 200, (
        "automation exhausted the shared bucket and locked out human users (02.12)"
    )
```

- **Alert on clients approaching their quota**, and tell them. A proactive email at 80% sustained usage
  prevents the ticket entirely and is far cheaper than handling it.
- **Review limits as a capacity decision, not a magic number.** Each limit should trace to a measured
  cost per request and a share of capacity you are willing to allocate (17.14).
- **Never classify traffic from a client-supplied header.** The class must derive from the credential,
  or clients will label their batch jobs as interactive.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...one client exceeds its limit? | `429` with `Retry-After` and `RateLimit-*`. Without `Retry-After`, a retry storm (01.3). |
| ...automated traffic exhausts a shared bucket? | Human users are locked out — the incident above. Separate quota classes. |
| ...a tenant issues ten API keys? | With a per-key limit only, they get 10× capacity. Enforce a tenant quota above the key burst. |
| ...the client is behind a corporate NAT? | IP limits punish thousands of legitimate users. Limit on authenticated identity where possible (01.10). |
| ...the rate-limit store (Redis) is down? | Decide **fail-open** (available, unprotected) or **fail-closed** (protected, unavailable) per class — critical fails open, automation fails closed is a defensible split (10.6). |
| ...requests differ in cost by 50×? | A uniform limit is meaningless. Use cost weighting. |
| ...you are overloaded despite limits? | That is load shedding, not rate limiting: `503`, not `429` (15.17). |
| ...a client ignores `Retry-After`? | Refusals must be cheap and enforced at the edge, or the storm consumes real capacity (01.10). |
| ...traffic increases 10×? | Per-client limits hold; aggregate capacity is a separate problem needing shedding and scaling. |

## Common Pitfalls

**One bucket for all of a tenant's traffic.** The incident above. *Instead:* quota classes derived from
the credential type.

**Emitting `RateLimit-*` only on `429`.** Every client then discovers its limit by hitting it.
*Instead:* headers on every response, so clients can self-pace.

**`429` without `Retry-After`.** 01.3's incident. *Instead:* always, and honour it in your own SDKs.

**Fixed windows with no smoothing.** A client burns the window in a minute and waits 59. *Instead:* a
token bucket or sliding window (10.13).

**Limiting by IP for authenticated traffic.** Punishes NAT'd users and is spoofable if proxy trust is
wrong (01.10). *Instead:* limit on the authenticated principal.

**Returning `429` when you are overloaded.** Blames the client for your capacity problem and tells them
to reduce a rate that was never the issue. *Instead:* `503` for shedding.

**Undocumented limits.** A limit nobody can look up is not a contract. *Instead:* publish per plan, per
class, with the window.

**Classifying traffic from a client-supplied header.** Clients will label everything interactive.
*Instead:* derive the class from the credential.

**Expensive refusals.** Under a storm, `429`s become your hot path. *Instead:* enforce crude limits at
the edge and precise ones in the app.

## How to Test This

- The class-isolation test above — the property the design exists to provide.
- A header test: assert `RateLimit-Limit`, `-Remaining`, `-Reset` and `-Policy` on a `200`, and those
  plus `Retry-After` on a `429`.
- A cost-weighting test: assert an export consumes 50 units and a list consumes 1.
- A store-outage test: stop Redis and assert the documented fail-open/fail-closed behaviour per class.
- A concurrency test: 50 simultaneous requests against a limit of 10 allow exactly 10 (this needs a
  real store — 13.12).

## Interview Questions

1. *Explain.* What is the most important design decision in rate limiting? — The dimension: whoever
   shares a bucket shares a fate.
2. *Debug this.* One customer's human users all get `429` while their integration runs fine, and the
   platform is healthy. — A shared tenant bucket consumed by automation; segment by credential type.
3. *Explain.* Difference between `429` and `503`, and why it matters to a client. — Your allocation vs
   our capacity; they imply different client responses.
4. *Design.* Design limits for an API used by both a dashboard and server-side integrations. — Per-tenant
   quota classes derived from the credential, plus a per-key burst and cost weighting.
5. *Explain.* Why emit `RateLimit-*` on successful responses? — So clients pace themselves instead of
   discovering the limit by being refused.
6. *Design.* Your rate-limit store fails. What happens? — A deliberate per-class choice: critical
   fails open, automation fails closed; state it in the runbook.
7. *Explain.* When is a uniform per-request limit inadequate? — When request costs differ by an order
   of magnitude; then weight by cost.

## Key Takeaways

- A rate limit is a **contract about capacity**, and its hardest decision is the **dimension** — who
  shares a bucket, and therefore who shares a fate.
- Separate **quota classes** so automated traffic cannot starve interactive traffic. It is 15.17's
  bulkhead applied to quota, and it is the fix for the most common rate-limiting outage.
- Derive the class from the credential, never from a client-supplied header.
- Emit `RateLimit-*` on **every** response, not just refusals. Proactive pacing prevents far more
  `429`s than any retry policy.
- `429` with `Retry-After` and a `scope` telling the client *which* bucket refused them. Anything less
  is unactionable.
- `429` is "your allocation"; `503` is "our capacity". Do not blame the client for your overload.
- Refusals must be cheap. Enforce crude limits at the edge, precise ones in the application.
- Publish the limits. An unpublished limit is a surprise, not a contract.

## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

A query plan is the database's itinerary for answering a question: which tables to read, by which route, in which order, joined how. `EXPLAIN` shows the itinerary the planner chose and the arithmetic it used to choose it. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` also makes the trip and writes down, at every step, what actually happened — how many rows came out, how long each step took, how many pages it touched. Reading a plan is comparing the forecast to the log book, step by step, and finding where they disagree; that disagreement is almost always where the trouble is.

The trap 05.6 exists to remove is believing the log book covers the whole journey. It does not. It stops at the instant the last row leaves the executor. Turning rows into bytes, pushing bytes down a socket, the driver turning bytes back into objects — none of that is on the page. 05.5 used two lines of a plan as a diagnostic; this notebook teaches the whole page, and then the edge of the page.

### Technical Explanation

**The tree.** A plan is a tree of **nodes**; leaves read data (`Seq Scan`, `Index Scan`, `Bitmap Heap Scan`) and interior nodes transform it (`Sort`, `Hash Join`, `Nested Loop`, `Aggregate`, `Limit`). Execution is a **pull model**: the root asks its child for a row, which asks its child, down to a leaf; time and rows flow upward. So a plan is read *bottom-up for cause* and *top-down for cost*: the leaves explain where rows came from, the root's numbers are the totals.

**The four numbers per node.** `(cost=0.42..3253.36 rows=1991 width=38)` — **startup cost** (work before the first row can be returned; a `Sort` has a high one, an `Index Scan` near zero), **total cost** (all rows), the estimated **row count**, and the average **row width** in bytes. Cost is not time and not I/O. It is arithmetic over five GUCs (`seq_page_cost` 1, `random_page_cost` 4, `cpu_tuple_cost` 0.01, `cpu_index_tuple_cost` 0.005, `cpu_operator_cost` 0.0025) and the statistics in `pg_class` (`relpages`, `reltuples`) and `pg_stats` (most-common values with frequencies, a histogram, `n_distinct`, physical `correlation`). The How It Works section derives one cost by hand and matches the planner to the cent — worth doing once, because after that you never again read a cost as a millisecond. Its only meaning is *relative to the other plans considered for this query*; comparing costs across two different queries is comparing two currencies without a rate.

**With `ANALYZE`, three more numbers.** `(actual time=0.012..0.875 rows=1000.00 loops=1)` — time to first row and to last row in milliseconds, actual rows, and **loops**: how many times this node was executed. On the inner side of a `Nested Loop` the node runs once per outer row, and **`rows` and `time` are per-loop averages** — the total is `rows × loops`. PostgreSQL 18 prints fractional actual rows (`rows=2.50`) precisely because the average is what is being reported. A node that reads "rows=1, time=0.003" is the most expensive node in the plan if `loops=40000`.

**Estimates and where they come from.** The planner estimates `rows` for a predicate by looking the value up in the column's most-common-values list (or the histogram), and for a conjunction of predicates it **multiplies selectivities as if the columns were independent**. Deskhub's `tenant_id` and `assignee_id` are not independent — an agent belongs to exactly one tenant — so `WHERE tenant_id = hanse AND assignee_id = X` is estimated at 410,000 × P(hanse) × P(X) ≈ 12 rows when the truth is 1,000. The gap itself is the finding; the plan above that node (nested loop or hash, index or seq) was chosen on the lie. Repairing estimates (`ANALYZE` targets, extended statistics) is 05.7's job; *spotting* them is this notebook's.

**Buffers and memory.** `Buffers: shared hit=5211 read=12101` counts 8 KiB pages served from PostgreSQL's shared buffer cache versus fetched from outside it (the OS page cache or disk — the plan cannot tell which). `temp read/written` is spill: a `Sort` that reports `external merge  Disk: 12848kB` or a `Hash` with `Batches: 2` ran out of `work_mem` and used temporary files. PostgreSQL 18 turns `BUFFERS` on by default whenever you say `ANALYZE`.

**What `ANALYZE` executes and what it skips.** `ANALYZE` runs the query. For `SELECT` that means real reads and real lock waits (which *do* show up inside the node's actual time); for `INSERT`/`UPDATE`/`DELETE` it means real writes and real WAL. What it *does not* do is produce output: the result rows are discarded at the top of the tree, so columns no node needs are never detoasted, decompressed or converted to their wire format, and nothing is sent to a client. PostgreSQL 17 added `SERIALIZE` to include that step, and the `Serialization:` line it prints is the bridge between the plan's clock and the application's.

**The three clocks**, then, which this notebook's incident turns on: the plan's `Execution Time` (executor only), the server-logged `duration` from `log_min_duration_statement` or `auto_explain` (executor **plus** output conversion and pushing bytes into the socket), and the driver's stopwatch around the call (all of that plus the wire, decoding, and — 05.9's subject — waiting for a pooled connection). When the three agree, the plan is the whole story. When they disagree, the gap between them names the layer.

### Mental Model

> `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` is a **stopwatch on the executor, not on the request**. Read a plan as a forecast beside a log book: find the lowest node where `rows` was wrong or `loops` was large — everything above it inherited the error — and then ask what the stopwatch never saw.

## How It Works

Every transcript below is from `_lab/lab_05_06_explain.py` against the module compose stack (PostgreSQL 18.6): Nordwind at 400k tickets and 60 agents, two small tenants at 5k tickets and 5 agents each, ~1.2M comments.

**1. Cost is arithmetic — prove it once.** `EXPLAIN` without `ANALYZE`, parallelism disabled so the scan is one node:

```text
Seq Scan on tickets  (cost=0.00..13845.00 rows=44444 width=0)
  Filter: (status = 'pending'::text)

pg_class: relpages=8720  reltuples=410000
cost = pages·seq_page_cost + tuples·cpu_tuple_cost + tuples·cpu_operator_cost
     = 8720·1.0 + 410000·0.01 + 410000·0.0025 = 13845.00
rows = reltuples · MCV_freq('pending') = 410000 · 0.1084 = 44444
```

Every term is visible: one sequential page cost per page, one tuple cost per row, one operator cost per row for the single filter. The row estimate is the table's row count times the frequency of `'pending'` in the most-common-values list. With parallelism back on, the same scan splits across two workers plus the leader:

```text
->  Parallel Seq Scan on tickets  (cost=0.00..10855.42 rows=18518 width=0)
parallel divisor for 2 workers = 2 + (1 − 0.3·2) = 2.4
8720 + (410000·0.01 + 410000·0.0025) / 2.4 = 10855.42
```

The disk term is *not* divided (every page is still read once), the CPU terms are divided by 2.4 (two workers plus a leader who spends 40% of its time coordinating), and `rows` per worker is the total over 2.4. Two things to carry: the planner's numbers are reproducible arithmetic over `pg_class` and `pg_stats`, so stale statistics produce confidently wrong plans; and the plan's `rows` on a parallel node is a per-worker figure, multiplied back up at `Gather`.

**2. Forecast versus log book — and `loops`.** One HanseFracht agent's tickets joined to their comments:

```text
Nested Loop  (cost=0.85..7672.13 rows=39 width=36)
             (actual time=0.040..3.901 rows=2500.00 loops=1)
  ->  Index Scan using uq_tickets_tenant_id_id on tickets t
          (cost=0.42..7536.85 rows=16 width=40)
          (actual time=0.012..0.875 rows=1000.00 loops=1)
        Index Cond: (tenant_id = '01a0…4d62dcb050b9'::uuid)
        Filter: (assignee_id = '01a0…a81d64f860c9'::uuid)
        Rows Removed by Filter: 4000
  ->  Index Scan using ix_comments_tenant_ticket on comments c
          (cost=0.42..8.45 rows=1 width=60)
          (actual time=0.002..0.003 rows=2.50 loops=1000)
        Index Cond: ((tenant_id = '01a0…'::uuid) AND (ticket_id = t.id))
        Index Searches: 1000
Execution Time: 3.992 ms
```

Read it bottom-up. The tickets node forecast 16 rows and produced 1,000 — a 62× miss from the independence assumption (tenant × agent). The comments node reports `rows=2.50 loops=1000`: it ran a thousand times, returning two and a half rows on average, 2,500 in total — which is exactly what the `Nested Loop` above reports. The per-loop time, 0.003 ms, is meaningless alone; times a thousand it is 3 ms, three quarters of the query. At this scale the misestimate is harmless; at 400k rows the same 62× miss is the difference between a nested loop the planner thought would run 16 times and one that runs a million.

**3. The node zoo, by selectivity.** One table, one index, one query shape, three time windows:

| Window | Node chosen | Actual rows | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| last 6 hours | `Index Scan using ix_tickets_tenant_activity` | 1,659 | 0.76 ms |
| last 3 days | `Bitmap Heap Scan` ← `Bitmap Index Scan` (`Heap Blocks: exact=423`) | 19,936 | 7.4 ms |
| last 30 days | `Parallel Seq Scan` (`Rows Removed by Filter: 70206` per worker) | 199,382 | 66 ms |

The planner is choosing an access method from the **estimated fraction** of the table: for a few rows, follow the index and pay one random heap page per row; for a few percent, collect all matching page numbers first (the bitmap), then read each page once, in physical order; for most of the table, read it all sequentially and parallelise. None of these is a bad plan — each is the right plan inside its fraction and wrong outside it — which is why a plan that "switched to a seq scan" is a *symptom* (the estimate crossed a threshold) rather than a diagnosis. The fourth scan type, `Index Only Scan … Heap Fetches: 0`, is 05.5's visibility-map promise holding: the count over `ix_tickets_inbox` touched 52 buffers and no heap at all.

**4. Buffers: whose cache?** A seq scan over `comments` (131 MiB, against 128 MB of `shared_buffers`), twice in a row:

```text
seq scan #1:  Buffers: shared hit=12151 read=5193    Execution Time: 110 ms
seq scan #2:  Buffers: shared hit=12402 read=4910    Execution Time:  99 ms
```

The second pass still reads a third of the table from outside shared buffers. That is deliberate: a sequential scan over a table larger than a quarter of `shared_buffers` runs through a small **ring buffer** so that it cannot evict the real working set to cache itself. `read` therefore means "not in PostgreSQL's cache", not "from disk" — the OS page cache is invisible to the plan — and a hit ratio on a big scan is a design choice, not a health metric.

**5. Memory lines — and reading the time next to them.** The same two plans under `work_mem` of 4 MB and 64 MB (`SET LOCAL`, parallelism off so the hash lives in one node):

```text
ORDER BY subject, 400k rows (collation en_US.utf8)
  4MB   Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 12848kB    Execution Time: 2025 ms
        Buffers: … temp read=1606 written=1612
  64MB  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 27914kB      Execution Time: 1821 ms
… the same sort under COLLATE "C"
  4MB   Sort Method: external merge  Disk: 18592kB    Execution Time:  388 ms
  64MB  Sort Method: quicksort  Memory: 34156kB      Execution Time:  369 ms

hash join, open/pending working set → comments
  4MB   Buckets: 131072  Batches: 2  Memory Usage: 4923kB   temp read=3572 written=3572
        Execution Time: 1087 ms
  64MB  Buckets: 131072  Batches: 1  Memory Usage: 8822kB   Execution Time:  560 ms
```

The plan tells you *what* happened — `external merge`, `Batches: 2`, `temp written` — and it is honest about it. Whether it *mattered* is in the time column, and here it barely did: across runs the in-memory quicksort of this sort was no faster than the spill (0.9–1.8 s spilled against 1.8–2.2 s in memory; a 28 MB array of pointers thrashes the CPU cache where 4 MB runs do not, and the temp files never left the OS page cache). The collation, meanwhile, cost five times more than the spill ever did: a locale-aware comparison on 400k `'Ticket #…'` strings whose abbreviated keys all collide. The hash join is the textbook case — one batch instead of two, half the time — and the point generalises: `work_mem` is allocated **per sort or hash node, per query**, not per connection, so raise it for the role or statement that needs it and never globally (a 64 MB default times 200 connections times a few nodes each is how a database runs out of memory at 03:00; 17.x prices it).

**6. The plan your application runs.** asyncpg prepares every statement (04.3), and PostgreSQL's plan cache decides per prepared statement whether to keep planning with the real parameter values (a **custom plan**) or switch to one plan for all values (a **generic plan**). The inbox query, with the status list as a parameter, seven runs on one connection:

```text
run 5:   3.49 ms  custom_plans=5 generic_plans=0
run 6:   3.87 ms  custom_plans=6 generic_plans=0      <- pg_prepared_statements
run 7:   2.09 ms  custom_plans=7 generic_plans=0

EXPLAIN (GENERIC_PLAN) …  status = ANY($3::text[]) …      -- PG16+, psql
  Limit  (cost=0.42..627.17 rows=50 width=38)
    ->  Index Scan using ix_tickets_tenant_activity on tickets  (cost=0.42..24054.97 …)
          Index Cond: (tenant_id = $1)
          Filter: ((assignee_id = $2) AND (status = ANY ($3)))
custom plan for the same parameters:
  Limit  (cost=0.42..82.11 rows=50 width=38)
    ->  Index Scan using ix_tickets_inbox on tickets  (cost=0.42..3253.36 …)
```

The generic plan cannot use `ix_tickets_inbox` at all: with `$3` unknown, the planner cannot prove `status = ANY($3)` implies the partial index's `WHERE status IN ('open','pending')`, so it falls back to the activity index plus a `Filter` — 05.5's incident plan. After five custom runs the cache compares the generic plan's *estimated* cost (627) with the average custom cost (82) and keeps re-planning. That protected Deskhub here by arithmetic, not by guarantee; the lab forces the other outcome for the sparse agent:

```text
SET plan_cache_mode = force_generic_plan;  PREPARE inbox(uuid, uuid, text[]) AS …
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE) EXECUTE inbox(…sparse agent…)
  ->  Index Scan using ix_tickets_tenant_activity  (actual time=262.336..262.788 rows=3.00)
        Rows Removed by Filter: 399997        Buffers: shared hit=2383 read=8098
Execution Time: 262.844 ms
SET plan_cache_mode = force_custom_plan;
  ->  Index Scan using ix_tickets_inbox  (actual time=0.039..0.042 rows=3.00)
Execution Time: 0.055 ms
```

The 18 January cliff, in disguise: same statement, same parameters, 5,000× apart, and `EXPLAIN` in psql with literals would show only the fast one. The two tells are `pg_prepared_statements.generic_plans` on a live connection and `EXPLAIN (GENERIC_PLAN)`; the knob is `plan_cache_mode`, and the design answer is to keep the partial index's predicate as a literal in the SQL (05.3 closed the status vocabulary; the query builder can emit the literal list) so provability never depends on the plan kind.

**7. `auto_explain`: the plans you did not think to explain.** Loaded per session in the lab (`LOAD 'auto_explain'`; in production via `shared_preload_libraries`), with `log_min_duration = '5ms'`, `log_analyze = on`, `log_timing = off`:

```text
LOG:  duration: 40.540 ms  plan:
	Query Text: SELECT count(*) FROM tickets WHERE tenant_id = $1 AND status = 'pending'
	Query Parameters: $1 = '01a06af6-f570-753f-ae58-c8bbdc092a6d'
	              ->  Parallel Seq Scan on tickets  (cost=0.00..11282.50 rows=18042 width=0) (actual rows=14921.67 loops=3)
	                    Filter: ((tenant_id = '…'::uuid) AND (status = 'pending'::text))
	                    Rows Removed by Filter: 121745
```

The sub-millisecond primary-key lookup issued right after it was not logged. What this buys over psql is the line `Query Parameters:` — the plan *with the values that were actually slow*, which for parameter-dependent cliffs (05.5, section 6 above) is the only plan worth having. `log_timing = off` matters: per-node timing is the expensive half of `ANALYZE` (clock reads on every row), and `auto_explain.sample_rate` limits how many statements pay for it at all.

**8. What `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` does not measure.** The ticket-timeline query for one Nordwind ticket — 43 comments — three ways:

```text
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE):
  Index Scan using ix_comments_tenant_ticket on comments c
      (actual time=0.024..0.046 rows=43.00 loops=1)    Buffers: shared hit=9
  Execution Time: 0.078 ms

the server's own log of the same fetch from asyncpg:
  LOG:  duration: 103.735 ms  execute __asyncpg_stmt_23__
  LOG:  duration: 103.717 ms  plan:
        Index Scan using ix_comments_tenant_ticket …  (actual time=0.023..0.273 rows=43.00 loops=1)

the driver's stopwatch around conn.fetch():
  290.4 ms for 43 rows, 13.6 MiB of body
```

Three clocks, three answers, one query. The plan's executor time is 0.078 ms; the server says the *execute* message took 103 ms and prints, in the same log entry, a plan whose nodes account for 0.27 ms of it; the application waited 290 ms. `SERIALIZE` closes the first gap:

```text
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, SERIALIZE):
  Index Scan … (actual time=0.122..0.439 rows=43.00 loops=1)   Buffers: shared hit=5
  Serialization: time=39.129 ms  output=13930kB  format=text
    Buffers: shared hit=500
  Execution Time: 39.690 ms
```

Now the plan admits to 13.9 MB of output and 500 buffer reads the scan never made — those are the **TOAST** pages: 40 of the 43 comments carry ~300 KB bodies that live out of line, and the executor never touched them because nothing in the plan needed their contents. Serialising them means fetching, decompressing and converting 13.6 MiB; sending them means pushing that through a socket; the remaining ~190 ms on the client is the wire and asyncpg decoding 13.6 MiB into Python strings. The plan was never the problem, and no `EXPLAIN` variant without `SERIALIZE` could have said so.

> **Version note** — captured on PostgreSQL 18.6 via the module compose stack. `BUFFERS` is on by default with `ANALYZE` from 18 (17 and earlier print buffers only when asked); fractional actual rows (`rows=2.50`) and `Index Searches` are 18 additions. `SERIALIZE` and `MEMORY` arrived in 17, `GENERIC_PLAN` in 16, `Query Parameters` in `auto_explain` (`log_parameter_max_length`) in 13. On 16 and earlier the serialization gap in section 8 is invisible to every `EXPLAIN` form and only the log/driver clocks reveal it. Reproduce with `_lab/lab_05_06_explain.py`.

## Code Example

Three pieces the incident leaves behind in Deskhub's codebase. First, the plan-reading helper that 05.5's CI test called `explain_json`, now reading the numbers this notebook taught — as a `FORMAT JSON` walk rather than a regex over text:

```python
# tests/schema/plans.py - read plans as data (FORMAT JSON), not as text
from typing import Any

type Plan = dict[str, Any]   # one node of EXPLAIN (FORMAT JSON) output

async def explain_json(conn, sql: str, *params, serialize: bool = False) -> Plan:
    """Run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS, FORMAT JSON [, SERIALIZE]) inside a
    transaction that is always rolled back: ANALYZE executes, and a DML
    shape registered here would otherwise write for real (05.6 §8)."""
    opts = "ANALYZE, BUFFERS, FORMAT JSON" + (", SERIALIZE" if serialize else "")
    async with conn.transaction():
        raw = await conn.fetchval(f"EXPLAIN ({opts}) {sql}", *params)
        await conn.execute("ROLLBACK")          # nothing an EXPLAIN did survives
    return __import__("json").loads(raw)[0]

def nodes(plan: Plan):
    """Depth-first walk of the tree - leaves first, so 'lowest wrong node' is
    'first node this yields whose estimate is off'."""
    root = plan["Plan"] if "Plan" in plan else plan
    for child in root.get("Plans", []):
        yield from nodes(child)
    yield root

def worst_estimate_ratio(plan: Plan) -> tuple[float, str]:
    """max(actual/estimated, estimated/actual) over the tree, with the node.
    Actual rows are per-loop averages: multiply by loops before comparing."""
    worst = (1.0, "")
    for n in nodes(plan):
        est, act = max(n["Plan Rows"], 1), n["Actual Rows"] * n["Actual Loops"]
        ratio = max(act / est, est / max(act, 1))
        if ratio > worst[0]:
            worst = (ratio, n["Node Type"] + " on " + n.get("Relation Name", "-"))
    return worst

def rows_removed_by_filter(plan: Plan) -> int:
    return sum(n.get("Rows Removed by Filter", 0) * n["Actual Loops"] for n in nodes(plan))

def serialized_bytes(plan: Plan) -> int:
    return plan.get("Serialization", {}).get("Output Volume", 0) * 1024
```

Two decisions to see: the walk yields **leaves first**, so the first node with a bad ratio is the lowest one — the lie the rest of the plan inherited — rather than the root that merely repeats it; and every actual count is multiplied by `Actual Loops` before comparison, because a `rows=2.5 loops=1000` node is a 2,500-row node. The `ROLLBACK` inside the transaction is not paranoia: the registry will one day contain an `UPDATE` shape.

Second, the shape tests that use it — 05.5's registered-shapes test, extended with the two budgets this incident needed:

```python
# tests/schema/test_list_queries_ride_their_indexes.py (05.6 additions)
LIST_QUERY_SHAPES = [
    ("inbox",           INBOX_SQL,    {"max_filtered": 100, "max_output_kib": 64}),
    ("ticket_timeline", TIMELINE_SQL, {"max_filtered": 0,   "max_output_kib": 256}),
]

@pytest.mark.parametrize("name,sql,budget", LIST_QUERY_SHAPES)
async def test_shape_is_bounded_end_to_end(db_at_scale, name, sql, budget):
    """27 Jan: a 0.078 ms plan behind a 2.4 s endpoint. The plan test passed
    because it measured the executor; the bytes were never on the page."""
    plan = await explain_json(db_at_scale, sql, *worst_case_params(name), serialize=True)
    assert rows_removed_by_filter(plan) <= budget["max_filtered"], (
        f"{name}: filters instead of seeking (05.5)")
    ratio, node = worst_estimate_ratio(plan)
    assert ratio < 50, f"{name}: estimate off {ratio:.0f}x at {node} - fix stats (05.7)"
    assert serialized_bytes(plan) <= budget["max_output_kib"] * 1024, (
        f"{name}: response volume {serialized_bytes(plan) // 1024} KiB - "
        f"the plan is fine and the endpoint is not (05.6)")
```

`worst_case_params("ticket_timeline")` returns the fixture ticket with forty 300 KB comments — the incident's shape, institutionalised the same way 05.5 institutionalised the sparse agent. The output budget is asserted from the plan's own `Serialization` block, on the scale seed, so the number is what production would ship, not what a ten-row fixture would.

Third, the query-side fix and its constraint. The timeline list never needed the body:

```sql
-- repositories/comments.py: TIMELINE_SQL after 27 Jan.
-- A preview and the stored size for the list; the body comes from
-- GET /v1/comments/{id} on expand (02.5's "page the data" rule, applied
-- to columns). left() on a compressed TOAST value decompresses only the
-- prefix it needs (PG13+); pg_column_size reads the stored size without
-- detoasting at all.
SELECT c.id, c.author_id, left(c.body, 200) AS preview,
       pg_column_size(c.body) AS stored_bytes, c.created_at
FROM comments c
WHERE c.tenant_id = $1 AND c.ticket_id = $2
ORDER BY c.created_at
LIMIT 50;
-- measured: Serialization: output=12kB, 0.54 ms; client fetch 9.8 ms (was 290)

-- deskhub/schema.sql (05.6 addition): a comment is a human-sized message.
-- Anything larger is an attachment (module 09). NOT VALID first: forty
-- existing dumps violate it and are backfilled into attachments, then VALIDATE.
ALTER TABLE comments
    ADD CONSTRAINT ck_comments_body_size CHECK (octet_length(body) <= 65536)
    NOT VALID;
```

⚠️ The constraint is the part people skip. Without it the lean projection is a fix for *this* endpoint, and the next one that selects `body` for a list — the export, the search indexer, the email digest — reproduces the incident in a place nobody EXPLAINed. 05.3's rule was "inputs closed": the database is the one validator every write path shares, and a 300 KB comment is an input the product never meant to accept. The lab shows both halves: `VALIDATE` refuses while the dumps exist (`check constraint "ck_comments_body_size" of relation "comments" is violated by some row`), and the next dump is refused *by name* — `ck_comments_body_size` — which 03.8's mapping turns into a 422 the ERP connector can act on.

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### Where to get a plan from

| Source | Shows | Costs | Use when | Not when |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `EXPLAIN` in psql, literals | The plan the planner picks *for those literals*, with `rows` estimates | Nothing runs | Designing an index; checking a rewrite | The app uses prepared statements (custom vs generic), a different role (RLS, `SET`tings), or you need actuals |
| `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` | Estimates beside actuals; buffers; memory lines | **Executes** the query, plus per-row clock reads | Diagnosing one slow statement with its worst-case parameters | On DML outside a transaction; on a statement whose cost *is* the problem (it runs again) |
| `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, SERIALIZE)` | Plus output volume and conversion time | Executes and serialises (not sent) | The plan is fast and the endpoint is not | PostgreSQL 16 or earlier — use the log/driver clocks |
| `EXPLAIN (GENERIC_PLAN)` | The plan a prepared statement gets once the cache goes generic | Nothing runs | Any query with a partial index or skewed column behind a parameter | You already checked `pg_prepared_statements` and it says `generic_plans = 0` on real connections |
| `auto_explain` | Plans of statements over a threshold, **with actual parameters** | Per-statement overhead; heavy with `log_timing` and `log_buffers`; log volume | Parameter-dependent cliffs; the statement you did not think to explain | Without `sample_rate` on a hot path; with `log_timing = on` fleet-wide |
| `pg_stat_statements` (05.7) | Aggregates per normalised statement: calls, mean/total time, rows, buffers | Shared memory; small per-call overhead | Finding *which* statement; ratio alerts (p99/p50) | Finding *why* — it has no plans |

**Recommendation.** Design with psql `EXPLAIN`; diagnose with `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` at the worst-case parameter inside `BEGIN … ROLLBACK`; add `SERIALIZE` the moment the plan's time and the endpoint's time disagree; keep `auto_explain` on in production with `log_min_duration` around your p99 budget, `log_timing = off`, and `sample_rate` well under 1 on hot paths; find statements in `pg_stat_statements`. Change it when a statement is parameter-sensitive: then `auto_explain`'s `Query Parameters` line is the primary tool and psql is the last.

### Custom plans, generic plans, `plan_cache_mode`

- **`auto`** (default): five custom plans, then generic if its estimated cost is not worse than the custom average. *Advantage:* no re-planning cost on hot statements. *Failure mode:* section 6 — a generic plan that cannot prove a partial-index predicate or that assumes average selectivity on a skewed column (`tenant_id` is 97% one value) wins on estimate and loses on data.
- **`force_custom_plan`**: always plan with the values. *Costs* planning time per execution (hundreds of microseconds on these shapes — cheap next to a 262 ms wrong plan, real on a 20k-statements-per-second lookup). *Use* per role (`ALTER ROLE deskhub_app SET plan_cache_mode`) when the workload is skewed and plans are cheap.
- **`force_generic_plan`**: never look at the values. *Use* almost never; it exists for stable plans on uniform data where planning is the bottleneck.

The better answer is upstream of the knob: write predicates so the good plan does not depend on the values being visible — literal partial-index predicates, leading `tenant_id` columns (05.4) — and assert it with `GENERIC_PLAN` in CI.

### `work_mem`: global, role, or statement

Global `work_mem` is a memory *multiplier* (nodes × queries × connections), so the rule is the lowest safe default globally, a higher value for the reporting role that runs the nightly aggregates, and `SET LOCAL work_mem` inside the specific transaction that owns a known big sort. Section 5's numbers are the caveat: read the time before touching the knob at all — a spill that lands in the page cache costs little, and a collation costs more than most spills.

## Production Scenario

**Deskhub, Wednesday 27 January, 14:10.** Nordwind's ERP vendor shipped an upgrade on Tuesday evening. The ERP-side connector (05.3's sync) has a diagnostics mode that, when a sync step retries, posts the full payload of the attempt to the linked Deskhub ticket as an internal comment "so support can see what we sent". The upgrade turned it on. By Wednesday lunchtime ~1,800 ERP-linked tickets carry between five and forty such comments, each around 300 KB of `order=… sku=… status=retry` lines, and Nordwind's agents report that opening those tickets "hangs the page".

### Symptoms

- `http_server_request_duration_seconds{route="/v1/tickets/{id}/comments"}` p99 climbs from 45 ms to **2.4 s** between 12:30 and 14:00; the alert fires at 14:10. Split by tenant, only `nordwind` moves; split further, only tickets with an `external_ref` (ERP-linked).
- The trace waterfall for an affected request is one span: `db.query SELECT … FROM comments c WHERE c.tenant_id = $1 AND c.ticket_id = $2 …` at 1.9 s of a 2.1 s request, with the remaining 200 ms in JSON encoding. The driver-side `db_client_duration_seconds` histogram for that statement label shows the same shape.
- Database dashboards are calm: CPU 12%, no lock waits, `pg_stat_activity` shows the sessions in `active` for a few hundred milliseconds each and nothing waiting. The database host's network egress is up an order of magnitude (`# illustrative - not captured output`; the lab's 13.6 MiB per call is the mechanism at capture scale).
- Two API pods restart on memory at 14:32 — each affected request materialises ~14 MB of comment bodies in the worker, and Nordwind's agents refresh.

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert → metrics.** Route, tenant, ticket cohort: the slowness is scoped to one query shape on one class of tickets. The first hypothesis is the obvious one — a plan regression on `comments` — and the on-call runs `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` with an affected ticket's ids: `Index Scan using ix_comments_tenant_ticket`, 43 rows, 9 buffers, **`Execution Time: 0.078 ms`**. Perfect plan. Hypothesis eliminated — and with it, 05.5's toolkit, which is why this notebook exists.
2. **Logs.** `log_min_duration_statement` (set fleet-wide at 100 ms) has been logging the statement all morning: `duration: 103.735 ms  execute __asyncpg_stmt_23__`. `auto_explain` logs the same statement with the same duration and prints, in the same entry, a plan whose nodes account for 0.27 ms. **The log line disagrees with its own plan.** That contradiction is the pivot: whatever costs 100 ms happens inside the `execute` message but outside the plan tree — after the last row is produced.
3. **Trace → dependency health.** The driver's 1.9 s (lab: 290 ms) is larger still than the server's 103 ms, so a second layer is adding on top: bytes on the wire and decoding. The "lock wait" hypothesis dies twice — waits would appear *inside* a node's actual time, and `pg_stat_activity` shows none. The "pool exhaustion" hypothesis (05.9) dies because the server-side clock is already at 100 ms; pool wait would inflate only the client clock.
4. **The plan, asked the right question.** `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, SERIALIZE)`: `Serialization: time=39.129 ms  output=13930kB`, with **500 buffer hits** the scan never made — TOAST pages. `SELECT sum(octet_length(body))` for the ticket: 14 MB across 43 comments, 40 of them created since Tuesday 18:00 by the ERP integration user. Root cause in hand at 14:38.

### Root Cause

The ERP connector started writing ~300 KB payload dumps as ticket comments, and the timeline endpoint selects every comment's full body for the ticket. Each page load therefore detoasts, serialises and ships ~14 MB — work that happens *after* the executor produces its 43 rows, so the plan stayed at 0.078 ms while the statement took 100 ms on the server and two seconds end to end. Nothing about the plan changed; the bytes behind the rows did.

### Fix

*Mitigation (14:41):* the ERP connector's diagnostics posting is disabled on Deskhub's side by a feature flag on the inbound integration (`integrations.erp.accept_payload_comments = false` — the API returns 422 with a `body_too_large` code the connector already handles as "skip"); the timeline endpoint gets a hot patch that replaces bodies over 64 KiB with a truncated preview. p99 is back under 100 ms by 14:50.

*Permanent fix (Thursday):* the Code Example, in full — the lean timeline projection (preview + stored size, body on expand, `LIMIT 50` per 02.5); `ck_comments_body_size` added `NOT VALID`; a backfill job that moves the 40-odd thousand dumps into attachments (module 09's store) with a one-line comment linking each; `VALIDATE CONSTRAINT` once the backfill's auditor query returns zero oversize rows; and the connector changed to post payloads as attachments. The lean query measures at 0.54 ms executor time, 12 KB serialised, 9.8 ms on the client.

### Prevention

- **Three-clock reconciliation as an alert**: the driver-side `db_client_duration_seconds` p95 for a statement label versus its `auto_explain`-logged execution time. A ratio above ~10 pages, because it can only mean serialisation volume, wire, or pool wait — three problems that no plan will show and that all deserve a page.
- **A response-volume budget per endpoint**: `http_response_size_bytes` histogram per route, alerting at a p95 over 1 MB, and the `Serialization` budget in the shape test above. Bytes are a contract dimension like page size (02.5), and the plan test now asserts them on the scale seed.
- **The constraint** — the permanent, path-independent guard. `ck_comments_body_size` makes the incident unrepresentable for every future write path, not just the one that was patched.
- `auto_explain` stays on (`log_min_duration = '200ms'`, `log_timing = off`, `sample_rate = 0.1` on hot paths), because the contradiction in step 2 was the fastest signal on the board — faster than any dashboard — and it only exists if plans are being logged with their durations.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...the plan is sub-millisecond and the endpoint is slow? | Three clocks: plan time vs server-logged `duration` vs driver stopwatch. Server > plan → serialisation volume (`SERIALIZE`, TOAST). Driver > server → wire, decoding, or pool wait (05.9). |
| ...estimated and actual rows differ by 100× on one node? | That node is the lie every node above it inherited; check for correlated columns (independence assumption), stale stats, or expressions without stats. Spot it here, fix it in 05.7. |
| ...an inner node shows tiny numbers with a huge `loops`? | Multiply. `rows × loops` and `time × loops` are the node's totals; a nested loop over a misestimated outer is the classic shape (06.5's N+1 looks identical inside one statement). |
| ...`Sort Method: external merge` or `Batches: 2`? | The node spilled. Read the time before touching `work_mem`; if it matters, raise it for the role or `SET LOCAL` in the statement — never globally. |
| ...`EXPLAIN ANALYZE` is run on an `UPDATE`? | It executes: rows change, WAL is written (measured 6,667 rows, 673 KB of WAL), triggers fire. `BEGIN; EXPLAIN ANALYZE …; ROLLBACK;` — always. |
| ...psql's plan differs from what the app experiences? | Prepared statements (generic vs custom — `pg_prepared_statements`, `GENERIC_PLAN`), a different role (RLS quals, `ALTER ROLE … SET`), or different parameters. `auto_explain`'s `Query Parameters` line is the app's plan. |
| ...`ANALYZE` itself is too expensive on a hot statement? | `TIMING OFF` removes the per-row clock reads; `auto_explain.sample_rate` limits how many executions pay; `EXPLAIN` without `ANALYZE` still shows the estimate side. |
| ...`Workers Launched` is less than `Workers Planned`? | The parallel worker pool was exhausted (`max_parallel_workers`); the plan was costed for parallelism it did not get — cost and time diverge without any estimate being wrong. |
| ...`Buffers: read` dominates on a scan? | Pages outside shared buffers — cold cache, a working set larger than `shared_buffers`, or a big seq scan's ring buffer by design. It is PostgreSQL's cache, not the machine's. |
| ...`Heap Fetches` is large on an index-only scan? | Visibility map staleness — 05.5's vacuum promise; not a plan-reading problem. |

## Common Pitfalls

**Reading cost as time.** Cost is arithmetic over statistics and five GUCs (derived exactly in section 1); a cost of 13,845 is not 13 ms and not 13 s. *Instead:* compare costs only between plans for the same query; use `ANALYZE` for time.

**Reading inner-loop numbers as totals.** `rows=2.50 loops=1000` is 2,500 rows and the whole query's time, not 2.5 rows and nothing. *Instead:* multiply by `loops` before judging any node; the JSON helper does it for you.

**Explaining with literals what the app runs with parameters.** The partial inbox index is provable from a literal and not from `$3`; psql shows the good plan and the app may run the other. *Instead:* `GENERIC_PLAN`, `pg_prepared_statements`, `auto_explain` with parameters; keep provable predicates literal.

**Explaining as the superuser what the app runs as `deskhub_app`.** RLS policies do not apply to the superuser (05.4), so the plan you see has no policy qual and may pick a different index. *Instead:* `SET ROLE deskhub_app` (and `SET app.tenant_id`) before explaining.

**Running `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on DML outside a transaction.** It is not a dry run. *Instead:* `BEGIN … ROLLBACK`, and `WAL` if you want to see the write cost.

**Treating `Execution Time` as request time.** The incident. *Instead:* the three clocks; `SERIALIZE` when the first two disagree.

**Raising `work_mem` globally to fix one spill.** Memory per node per query per connection; the spill may not have been the cost anyway (section 5). *Instead:* measure, then per-role or `SET LOCAL`.

**Chasing the node with the largest self-reported time before checking estimates.** Time is a consequence; the misestimate below it is the cause, and fixing the cause changes the plan shape entirely. *Instead:* bottom-up for cause, top-down for cost.

**Logging every plan with `log_timing = on` in production.** The clock reads are the expensive half of `ANALYZE`; a hot OLTP path can slow by a noticeable fraction. *Instead:* `log_timing = off`, a `log_min_duration` near the p99 budget, `sample_rate`.

## Interview Questions

1. *Explain.* What is `cost` in a plan, and why can't you compare costs between two queries? — Unitless arithmetic over `relpages`/`reltuples`/`pg_stats` and five GUCs (derive `pages + rows·0.01 + rows·0.0025` for a seq scan); it ranks alternatives for one query, and shares no scale with another.
2. *Debug this.* A plan shows an index scan with `rows=1 loops=1` on the outer side and `rows=3 loops=1` on the inner, total 4 ms; the same query takes 900 ms in the app. — Parameters differ (generic plan or skew), role differs (RLS), or the time is off the page (serialisation, wire, pool). Check `Query Parameters` in `auto_explain`, then the three clocks.
3. *Explain.* What does `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` not measure, and how do you see it? — Output serialisation and detoasting, network, client decode, pool wait, and planning (shown separately). `SERIALIZE` (PG17+) for the first; server log `duration` and the driver histogram for the rest.
4. *Design.* A CI gate that catches plan regressions before deploy. — Registered shapes, scale seed, worst-case parameters; `FORMAT JSON` walk asserting `Rows Removed`, estimate ratio, and serialised volume; `GENERIC_PLAN` asserted for parameterised shapes; run in a rolled-back transaction.
5. *Explain.* Why can a prepared statement run a worse plan than the same SQL in psql? — After five custom plans the cache may choose a generic plan by *estimated* cost; a generic plan cannot prove partial-index predicates from `$n` or see skew. `pg_prepared_statements.generic_plans` and `plan_cache_mode`.
6. *Debug this.* `Sort Method: external merge Disk: 12 MB` on a report; the team wants `work_mem = 256MB` globally. — Read the time first (spill in page cache may be cheap; collation may dominate); if it matters, `ALTER ROLE reporting SET work_mem` or `SET LOCAL`; global is a multiplier across nodes × queries × connections.
7. *Explain.* Where do row misestimates come from, and how do you find the one that matters? — Independence assumption across correlated columns, stale or absent stats, expressions; walk the tree leaves-first and take the lowest node with a large ratio — everything above inherited it.
8. *Design.* Production plan observability for a multi-tenant API. — `auto_explain` sampled with parameters and `log_timing = off`; `pg_stat_statements` for *which*; driver-side per-statement histograms for the third clock; an alert on driver-time / plan-time ratio; RLS role in every diagnostic session.

## Key Takeaways

- A plan is **a forecast beside a log book**: cost and `rows` are arithmetic over statistics (derived exactly: 8720 + 4100 + 1025 = 13,845.00); `actual` is what happened. Read bottom-up for cause, top-down for cost, and find the lowest node where they disagree.
- **`rows` and `time` on a looped node are per-loop averages** — `rows=2.50 loops=1000` is 2,500 rows and most of the query. Multiply before judging.
- The node type is a function of the **estimated fraction** of the table (Index → Bitmap → Seq, measured at 1.7k / 20k / 200k rows); a "switch to seq scan" is a symptom of an estimate, not a diagnosis.
- Memory lines tell you *what* happened (`external merge`, `Batches: 2`); the time column tells you whether it mattered — here the collation cost 5× more than the spill. `work_mem` is per node per query: per role or `SET LOCAL`, never global.
- **The plan psql shows is not necessarily the plan your app runs**: generic plans cannot prove partial-index predicates from parameters (627 vs 82 estimated; 262 ms vs 0.055 ms forced); RLS roles carry quals the superuser never sees. `pg_prepared_statements`, `GENERIC_PLAN`, and `auto_explain`'s `Query Parameters` are how you see the real one.
- **`EXPLAIN ANALYZE` is a stopwatch on the executor, not the request.** 0.078 ms plan, 103 ms server-logged execute, 290 ms on the client, for 13.6 MiB of TOASTed bodies the plan never touched. `SERIALIZE` shows the first gap; the three clocks show the rest.
- `ANALYZE` means execute: DML inside `BEGIN … ROLLBACK`, always (673 KB of WAL for one "look"); `TIMING OFF` and sampling when the measurement is the cost.
- The incident's permanent fix was two things the plan could never have suggested: a **projection that ships what the list shows**, and a **constraint that closes the input** — the database as the one validator every write path shares.

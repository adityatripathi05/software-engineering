# 05.1 Relational Modelling and Key Choice — serial, UUIDv4, UUIDv7

> **Prerequisites:** modern-python 10.1 (SQL basics — assumed), 10.2 (transactions, who commits) · 02.1 (public identifiers vs storage keys) · 02.5 (total order for cursors) · 04.6 (constraints as arbiters)
> **What you'll learn:**
> - Turn a domain into tables and foreign keys, and defend every relationship's direction and optionality
> - Choose a natural or surrogate identity per table on criteria, not habit — and know why natural identities become UNIQUE constraints instead of primary keys
> - Predict, from B-tree mechanics, what a key type does to index size, WAL volume and replica lag — with the numbers measured, not asserted

> **Level:** Beginner

> ⚡ **Tuesday 15 December, 09:50** — Deskhub's biggest-ever tenant is importing seven years of ticket history, and every *other* tenant's p99 climbs while the read replica falls minutes behind. The cause: the primary key's random bits.

## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

New module, new machine room. Modules 05–07 run on **Deskhub**, a multi-tenant helpdesk: companies (*tenants*) sign up, their support staff (*agents*) work *tickets* raised by their customers, tickets accumulate *comments*, comments carry *attachments*, and the busiest thing the product does all day is render filtered, sorted ticket lists. Ledgerly retires as the running system; everything it taught — public ids (02.1), cursor pagination (02.5), constraints as arbiters (04.6) — rides along as references.

Relational modelling is deciding three things: which tables exist, how rows point at each other, and what identifies a row forever. The first two are mostly dictated by the domain once you name its nouns and their relationships. The third — the **primary key** — looks like the most boring decision on the page, a one-line default you copy from the last project. This notebook exists because that one line is actually two decisions in disguise: a *semantic* one (what does ordering by this key mean, and what does the key reveal to anyone who sees it?) and a *physical* one (where does each new row land in the index?). Get the physical one wrong and the bill arrives at exactly the moment you can least afford it — under your biggest write load ever, which is the incident below.

### Technical Explanation

**Modelling: nouns become tables, relationships become foreign keys.** A **foreign key** always points child→parent: a ticket carries `tenant_id` because a ticket cannot exist without its tenant; a comment carries `ticket_id`; an attachment carries `comment_id`. Optionality is expressed by nullability — `tickets.assignee_id` is a nullable FK, because an unassigned ticket is a legitimate state, not an error. Note the echo of 02.1's nesting rule: a comment's parentage is absolute (FK, `NOT NULL`), while a ticket's assignment is reassignable (nullable FK) — the same "can this relationship ever change?" question, now answered in DDL instead of URI design.

**Natural vs surrogate identity.** A **natural key** is an identity the domain already has: a tenant's `slug`, an agent's `(tenant_id, email)`, a ticket's human-facing `(tenant_id, number)`. A **surrogate key** is one the system invents. The rule that survives production: *record every natural identity as a `UNIQUE` constraint, and give the table a surrogate primary key anyway.* A primary key must be **immutable** (every FK in the system embeds it) and **meaningless** (so no business change can ever force a rename) — and natural keys fail both tests over time: emails change, slugs get rebranded, ticket numbers turn out to need per-tenant sequences with legal gap rules. The natural identity still does its real job — the database refuses duplicates, and 04.6 taught you that the constraint is the only arbiter that works across replicas — it just does that job from a `UNIQUE` constraint one line below the PK.

**The three surrogate candidates**, on the pinned stack (PostgreSQL 18):

- **`bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY`** — the modern spelling of what everyone still calls **serial** (`serial` itself is a legacy macro that creates a sequence and wires a default; identity columns are the SQL-standard replacement and what you should write today). 8 bytes, allocated from a **sequence**: strictly increasing, single-database, and *gappy by design* — a rolled-back insert consumes its number forever, captured below.
- **UUIDv4** (`gen_random_uuid()`) — 16 bytes, 122 random bits, generatable anywhere by anyone with no coordination. Its defining property is also its cost: a v4 value says *nothing* — not when, not where, not in what order.
- **UUIDv7** (`uuidv7()`, native in PostgreSQL 18; RFC 9562) — 16 bytes laid out as a **48-bit Unix-millisecond timestamp**, then 74 random bits. Still globally unique, still uncoordinated — but the timestamp prefix means v7 values *sort by creation time*, and the creation instant can be read back out with `uuid_extract_timestamp()`.

**The property everything else hangs on: does key order track insertion order?** Identity and v7: yes. v4: aggressively no. That single bit decides the B-tree geometry (How It Works), what `ORDER BY id` means, whether the key can serve as a cursor (02.5's total order, satisfied by uniqueness plus near-chronological order), and what the key leaks: an identity leaks your *volume* and invites enumeration (02.1's lesson — never expose it raw), a v7 leaks its row's *creation time* (usually fine for a ticket, whose creation time is on screen anyway; a real decision for anything more sensitive), and a v4 leaks nothing at all.

### Mental Model

> A primary key is **a name forever and an address today**. The name side wants immutable and meaningless — which is why natural identities live in UNIQUE constraints instead. The address side is about where the next row lands in the B-tree: identity and v7 file every new row at the *end of the book*; v4 files each one on a *random page*. One of these is a ledger; the other is a confetti cannon, and you pay for the confetti on every insert, forever.

## How It Works

**Where an insert lands.** A B-tree keeps keys sorted, eight-kilobyte page by page. An *ordered* key always arrives greater than every existing key, so it appends to the rightmost leaf — a fast path PostgreSQL optimises specifically — and when that leaf fills, the split leaves the old page ~90% full (the **90/10 split**: everything already there stays, growth continues to the right). A *random* key lands on an arbitrary leaf; when that leaf is full it must split **50/50**, moving half its entries to a new page. Do that a million times and the leaves converge to ~two-thirds full, in allocation order that has nothing to do with key order:

```text
identity / v7:  [p1 90%][p2 90%][p3 90%][p4 ...→]      every insert lands HERE ─┘
v4:             [p7 68%][p2 71%][p9 65%][p1 74%]...    every insert lands ANYWHERE,
                                                       any full page splits in half
```

Measured — one million rows per key type, identical payload, identical column widths (`_lab/lab_05_01_keys.py`):

```text
k_identity   insert   2.1s   wal   165.8 MiB   pkey    21.4 MiB   leaf_density  90.0%   fragmentation   0.0%
k_uuidv4     insert   4.3s   wal   192.9 MiB   pkey    37.8 MiB   leaf_density  71.7%   fragmentation  49.9%
k_uuidv7     insert   2.6s   wal   179.1 MiB   pkey    30.1 MiB   leaf_density  90.0%   fragmentation   0.0%
```

Same table, same rows: the v4 primary-key index is **76% larger** than identity's and 26% larger than v7's (v7 pays only for 16-byte entries, at full density), it is half-fragmented on day one, and the build took nearly twice as long. The `avg_leaf_density` numbers are the split arithmetic made visible: 90% is the rightmost-split optimisation; 71.7% is what 50/50 splits converge to.

**The steady-state bill is WAL.** PostgreSQL's crash safety works by writing every change to the **write-ahead log** first — and, the part that matters here, after each **checkpoint** the *first* modification to any 8 KiB page writes the entire page into WAL (a **full-page image**), so a torn write can be repaired. Now run the two insert patterns through that rule. Ordered keys touch the same handful of rightmost leaf pages over and over: a few FPIs, then cheap deltas. Random keys touch a *different leaf almost every insert* — which after every checkpoint means an 8 KiB image per touched page. Measured — `CHECKPOINT`, then the same 100,000 rows into each already-populated table:

```text
k_identity   insert   0.2s   wal    16.6 MiB
k_uuidv4     insert   0.5s   wal    46.3 MiB
k_uuidv7     insert   0.3s   wal    17.9 MiB
```

Same rows, ~3× the WAL — and this multiplier *grows* with the index, because a bigger index has more leaves to scatter across. WAL volume is not an accounting curiosity: it is the stream your replicas must replay (17.12) and your backups must retain (05.18). Random keys tax both, forever.

**And the semantic half.** `ORDER BY id DESC LIMIT 100` is the most natural query in a helpdesk — the newest work. Measured against the three tables, immediately after the second batch:

```text
k_identity   page spans rows created between     0.8s and   0.8s ago
k_uuidv4     page spans rows created between     8.2s and   0.3s ago
k_uuidv7     page spans rows created between     0.0s and   0.0s ago
```

Identity and v7 return a tight window of the newest rows; v4 returns a sample smeared across the table's entire history. The scan itself is equally cheap everywhere — `Buffers: shared hit=5` for the v7 backward index scan — which is exactly what makes the v4 version dangerous: nothing is slow, nothing errors, the *meaning* is simply gone. One nuance for 02.5's cursor discipline: v7 order is unique (so cursors are stable) but only millisecond-chronological — two rows keyed in the same millisecond may sort either way, which is fine for pagination and worth knowing before you treat v7 order as a commit log.

## Code Example

The founding schema — the running system for the next three modules, exactly as the lab creates it. Parents first:

```sql
-- deskhub/schema.sql (founding version; constraints deepen in 05.3, tenancy in 05.4)
CREATE TABLE tenants (
    id          uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    slug        text NOT NULL UNIQUE,          -- natural identity: constrained, not the PK
    name        text NOT NULL,
    created_at  timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

CREATE TABLE agents (
    id           uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    tenant_id    uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES tenants(id),
    email        text NOT NULL,
    display_name text NOT NULL,
    created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    UNIQUE (tenant_id, email)                  -- emails are unique PER TENANT, and they change:
);                                             -- exactly why they are not the primary key

CREATE TABLE tickets (
    id              uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    tenant_id       uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES tenants(id),
    number          bigint NOT NULL,           -- the human-facing "TKT-1042"; allocation is 05.3
    requester_email text NOT NULL,
    subject         text NOT NULL,
    status          text NOT NULL DEFAULT 'open',
    priority        text NOT NULL DEFAULT 'normal',
    assignee_id     uuid REFERENCES agents(id),      -- nullable: unassigned is a real state
    created_at      timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    updated_at      timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    UNIQUE (tenant_id, number)
);
```

Every modelling rule from the Concept section is visible here: surrogate v7 primary keys, every natural identity a `UNIQUE` constraint (`slug`, `(tenant_id, email)`, `(tenant_id, number)`), and relationship direction child→parent. The `number` column is the identity humans use — sequential *per tenant* because support conversations need "ticket 1042", allocated transactionally in 05.3 precisely because sequences cannot promise gaplessness (captured below). The two dependent tables complete the tree:

```sql
CREATE TABLE comments (
    id              uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    ticket_id       uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES tickets(id),   -- absolute parentage: NOT NULL
    author_agent_id uuid REFERENCES agents(id),             -- NULL = the requester wrote it
    body            text NOT NULL,
    is_internal     boolean NOT NULL DEFAULT false,
    created_at      timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

CREATE TABLE attachments (
    id           uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7(),
    comment_id   uuid NOT NULL REFERENCES comments(id),
    filename     text NOT NULL,
    content_type text NOT NULL,
    byte_size    bigint NOT NULL,
    storage_key  text NOT NULL,                -- the bytes live in object storage (module 09)
    created_at   timestamptz NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);
```

Exercised end to end, the schema's key choice pays a small dividend immediately — the id itself testifies to when the row was keyed:

```text
ticket id   : 01a051fe-b490-7fd3-9240-82bfa73f58a5
keyed_at    : 2026-08-30 09:27:22+00:00   (uuid_extract_timestamp reads it back)
subject     : 'Cannot download invoice PDF'  assignee='Anna Weber'  comments=1
```

Two captured behaviours complete the picture. First, what each generator actually promises about order — five rows inserted five milliseconds apart:

```text
arrival order      : [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]
ORDER BY v4 gives  : [3, 4, 2, 1, 5]   <- random bits sort in random order
ORDER BY v7 gives  : [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]   <- the timestamp prefix sorts by time
```

And the sequence gap, because someone on every team eventually files "missing ids" as a corruption bug:

```text
INSERT 'first'  -> id=1
INSERT 'doomed' -> ROLLBACK             (the row dies; nextval does not rewind)
INSERT 'third'  -> id=3
```

⚠️ Gaps are normal operation. A sequence hands out numbers *before* the transaction commits and never takes them back — rollbacks, crashes and sequence caching all leave holes. Anything that genuinely requires gapless numbering (invoice numbers with legal requirements, Deskhub's per-tenant ticket numbers) is a separate, transactional allocation built in 05.3 — never the primary key's job.

> **Version note** — captured on the module's `compose.yaml` (PostgreSQL 18.6): `uuidv7()` and `uuid_extract_timestamp()` are native in 18. On 17 and earlier, generate v7 in the application (`uuid_utils`/`uuid6` on PyPI) and the entire story is unchanged — the B-tree cares about the values, not who made them. `gen_random_uuid()` is core since 13. Reproduce everything with `_lab/lab_05_01_keys.py`.

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### The three key types

| | `bigint` identity ("serial") | UUIDv4 | UUIDv7 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Size (key; every FK repeats it) | 8 bytes | 16 bytes | 16 bytes |
| Index geometry (measured) | 90% density, appends | 72% density, 50% fragmented, ~3× steady-state WAL | 90% density, appends |
| `ORDER BY id` means | Insertion order | Nothing | Creation time (ms precision) |
| Generatable without the DB | ❌ needs the sequence | ✅ anywhere | ✅ anywhere |
| Safe across DBs/regions/merges | ❌ collides | ✅ | ✅ |
| What it leaks if exposed | Volume + enumeration (02.1) | Nothing | Creation timestamp |
| Use when | Internal-only tables, tight join loops where 8 bytes matter | Ids that must reveal *nothing*, or pre-date v7 | **The default for new tables** |

**Deskhub's decision: one key.** `uuid PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT uuidv7()` is both the storage key and (prefixed, per 02.1: `tck_01a051fe…`) the public identifier. One index, no join-time translation, cursor-friendly order, and safe to mint in the application or in a future second region. The cost is honest: 16-byte keys everywhere a FK lives, and a timestamp readable by anyone holding an id. **Ledgerly chose the other pattern** — internal `bigint` for join performance plus a separate public identifier column (02.1) — and both are defensible: choose two keys when join-heavy internal load dominates and ids must leak nothing; choose one v7 key when simplicity and orderability dominate, which is Deskhub's read-heavy list workload exactly. Change the decision the day measurement says the 8 extra bytes matter — and make it *early* if ever, because a primary key is embedded in every FK and every URL, and 02.1 taught what URLs cost to change.

### When a natural key IS the primary key

Pure association tables — `ticket_tags (ticket_id, tag_id, PRIMARY KEY (ticket_id, tag_id))` — need no surrogate: the pair *is* the row's whole identity, immutable by construction, and a surrogate would just be a third column nobody queries. The same goes for closed reference data keyed by external standards (`currencies(code)`, ISO country codes). The test is the same both times: is this identity genuinely immutable and meaningless-to-the-business? For anything with a life of its own, it never is.

### One misconception worth killing early

The advice "UUID primary keys bloat *every* index" is **InnoDB folklore, not PostgreSQL fact**. In MySQL's InnoDB the table is physically arranged by primary key and every secondary index stores the PK — so a fat random PK really does tax everything. In PostgreSQL, tables are heaps, and secondary indexes point at physical row addresses (TIDs), never at the PK. The real PostgreSQL costs of a v4 key are the three you measured: the PK index's own geometry, the WAL amplification, and the 16 bytes in each FK column and its index. Engineers who can name *which* database's storage model an argument comes from are the ones interviewers remember.

## Production Scenario

**Deskhub, Tuesday 15 December, 09:50.** Deskhub's largest-ever deal closed in November: a national logistics group replacing its legacy helpdesk. Today their migration begins — seven years of history, ~6M tickets and 19M comments, imported through the API at ~800 rows/second while every other tenant's Tuesday runs as normal. Deskhub launched two years ago with `gen_random_uuid()` primary keys throughout — "unguessable, standard practice", said the launch-era review.

At 09:50 two alerts fire together: ticket-create p99 fleet-wide (55 ms → 480 ms, every tenant, not just the importer) and replica lag on the read replica that serves agent inboxes (0 → 190 s and climbing — agents are now seeing three-minute-old ticket lists, the wrong-data flavour of this incident).

### Symptoms

- Insert-heavy endpoints degraded for **every tenant**; read endpoints on the primary normal. The importer's own throughput is also sagging.
- WAL generation: 4 MB/s → **26 MB/s** — while the row-insert rate is only ~3× normal. Bytes-of-WAL-per-row-inserted has roughly tripled: the module-02 ratio discipline, pointing away from "just more load".
- Postgres logs, every ~90 seconds: `LOG: checkpoints are occurring too frequently (87 seconds apart)` — the WAL volume is forcing early checkpoints, and each checkpoint resets the full-page-image debt, which generates more WAL (a feedback loop).
- The replica applies WAL slower than the primary now produces it; lag grows linearly. Cache hit ratio on `tickets_pkey` reads has fallen from 99% to 91% (`# illustrative - not captured output`; the mechanism is the lab's, at production scale).

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert → Metrics.** Fleet-wide insert degradation plus replica lag with healthy CPU rules out one slow query; the WAL-per-row ratio names the class: *each insert has become individually more expensive to log*, coinciding with the import.
2. **Logs.** The checkpoint warnings corroborate: WAL volume, not query cost, is the pressured resource.
3. **Look at the index, not the queries.** `pgstatindex('tickets_pkey')`: `avg_leaf_density 68%, leaf_fragmentation 51%` (`# illustrative - not captured output`) — the production twin of the lab's 71.7%/49.9% capture. The primary key is a v4 B-tree, ~46 GB against ~30 GB of actually-needed entries, and no longer close to fitting in cache.
4. **Confirm the mechanism** with this notebook's lab shape: random keys touch a different leaf per insert; after each (now-frequent) checkpoint, every first touch writes an 8 KiB full-page image. The import didn't just add rows — at triple the insert rate it multiplied *WAL bytes per row*, saturating checkpoint and replication machinery sized for the ordered-key bill. The measured 3×-and-growing multiplier from section D is the incident's arithmetic in miniature.
5. **Eliminated:** the import's queries themselves (plain INSERTs, well-formed), lock contention (no waits in `pg_stat_activity`), and the replica's hardware (apply-bound on random index pages, not I/O-saturated).

### Root Cause

Every Deskhub table keys on UUIDv4, so every insert lands on a random leaf of a large B-tree; the bulk import tripled the insert rate, but the random insertion points multiplied WAL output per row (full-page images of nearly every touched leaf after ever-more-frequent checkpoints), saturating WAL write, checkpoint and replica-apply capacity that ordered keys would barely have noticed — degrading every tenant's writes and knocking the inbox replica minutes into the past.

### Fix

*Mitigation (10:20):* pause the import; WAL rate and replica lag recover within minutes. Resume overnight at 200 rows/s in checkpoint-aligned batches — the tenant's go-live slips two days, which the account team prefers to a fleet-wide brownout.

*Permanent fix:* new keys become time-ordered — and the beautiful part is the size of the change:

```sql
ALTER TABLE tickets  ALTER COLUMN id SET DEFAULT uuidv7();
ALTER TABLE comments ALTER COLUMN id SET DEFAULT uuidv7();
-- same 16-byte uuid column type: no migration, no rewrite, no FK changes,
-- old v4 ids stay valid forever; only NEW rows change character.
```

The import restarts with client-side v7 ids minted in historical order (so seven years of history lands pre-sorted, appending). Once the import completes, one `REINDEX CONCURRENTLY tickets_pkey` repacks the accumulated v4 geometry (density back to ~90%; 05.11 covers why bloat needs an explicit repack). The lab's measured table — 76% index inflation, ~3× steady-state WAL — goes into the decision record as the justification.

### Prevention

- **The WAL-per-row ratio becomes a standing alert**: `rate(wal_bytes) / rate(tuples_inserted)` above 2× its baseline pages — it is the earliest, cheapest signal for this entire class (random keys, but also over-indexing and hot-update storms later in the module).
- **A nightly `pgstatindex` check** on the fleet's hottest primary keys: `avg_leaf_density < 75%` opens a ticket before it becomes an incident.
- **The lab harness is the CI decision record**: key-type changes to any high-write table must re-run it and paste the geometry table into the PR — measured, not folklore.
- **Bulk imports get their own lane**: rate-capped, off-peak, checkpoint-aware — 02.12's fate-sharing lesson applied to the write path.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...you need to change the PK type later? | Every FK, index and URL embeds it — the one modelling decision that is *early or never*. (The v4→v7 default swap worked precisely because the column type didn't change.) |
| ...two regions/databases generate ids concurrently? | UUIDs (v4 or v7) never collide in practice; sequences collide immediately. Multi-writer futures rule out bare identity keys. |
| ...the app must mint an id before the INSERT (offline clients, 02.8-style idempotency)? | UUIDs can be generated client-side; identity values cannot exist until the database speaks. |
| ...an id appears in a URL or email? | Identity leaks volume and invites enumeration (02.1); v7 leaks creation time; v4 leaks nothing. Expose prefixed ids either way (`tck_…`), and pick the leak you can live with. |
| ...business rules demand gapless numbers? | Sequences can't promise it (measured: rollback eats a number). A transactional per-tenant counter — 05.3 — owns that job. |
| ...two rows are keyed in the same millisecond? | v7 order between them is arbitrary (random bits break the tie). Unique, cursor-safe — but not a commit log. |
| ...write traffic ×10? | Ordered keys scale linearly; random keys multiply WAL per row as the index grows and blow the checkpoint/replication budget first — the incident. |
| ...a bulk import runs during business hours? | It shares WAL, checkpoint and replica capacity with everyone. Rate-cap it and watch the WAL-per-row ratio, whatever the key type. |

## Common Pitfalls

**Defaulting to UUIDv4 because "UUIDs are standard practice".** The advice predates v7: you get global uniqueness and pay confetti geometry — measured here at +76% index size and ~3× steady-state WAL. *Instead:* v7 by default; v4 only when the id must reveal nothing.

**Treating `ORDER BY id` as "newest first" under v4.** It runs fast, documents nothing wrong, and returns a random sample (measured: a 100-row page spanning the table's whole history). *Instead:* v7/identity keys, or an explicit `ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC` (02.5's total order).

**Natural primary keys.** `email` as the agents PK means an email change cascades through every FK; a composite natural PK spreads that pain to every child table. *Instead:* surrogate PK + `UNIQUE` on the natural identity — you keep the integrity, you lose the fragility.

**Exposing a sequence in public.** `/tickets/1042` tells competitors your volume and turns any authorisation gap into an enumerable breach — 02.1's incident, storage edition. *Instead:* per-tenant human numbers for humans, opaque prefixed ids in URLs.

**Filing sequence gaps as data loss.** Rollbacks and crashes eat numbers by design (measured: 1, then 3). *Instead:* document gaps as normal; build gapless allocation transactionally where genuinely required (05.3).

**Importing the InnoDB argument into Postgres reviews.** "UUID PKs bloat every secondary index" is a clustered-storage fact from a different engine. *Instead:* argue Postgres's real costs — PK geometry, WAL, FK width — with the lab's numbers.

**Timestamps as primary keys.** `created_at` collides under concurrency and invites "fix" hacks. *Instead:* v7 — a timestamp *plus* 74 collision-breaking bits, which is exactly the fix done right.

## How to Test This

- The lab harness (`_lab/lab_05_01_keys.py`) as a standing decision record: re-run on any proposed key change to a high-write table; the geometry table goes in the PR.
- A schema test: every table's PK default is `uuidv7()` (query `pg_attrdef` in CI), so a copy-pasted `gen_random_uuid()` is a failing build, not a 2-years-later incident.
- A pagination test on v7 ids: insert concurrently, cursor through by `id`, assert no skips or repeats (02.5's drift test, now keyed on the PK).
- The nightly `pgstatindex` density check, alerting below 75% on hot primary keys.

## Interview Questions

1. *Explain.* Why do ordered and random primary keys produce different B-tree geometry? — Rightmost inserts append and split 90/10 (pages ~90% full); random inserts split 50/50 everywhere (pages converge to ~⅔ full, allocation-order fragmented). Same rows, ~76% fatter index, measured.
2. *Explain.* Where does the WAL amplification of random keys come from? — After each checkpoint, the first modification of any page logs a full-page image; random keys touch a different leaf per insert, so nearly every insert pays 8 KiB. Measured ~3× for identical rows, growing with index size.
3. *Debug this.* During a bulk import, every tenant's insert p99 degrades, WAL-per-row triples, checkpoints fire every 90 s, and the replica falls minutes behind. — Random-key B-tree under tripled insert rate; confirm with `pgstatindex` density and the WAL ratio; mitigate by pausing the import, fix by moving new keys to v7.
4. *Design.* Choose keys for a multi-tenant helpdesk with heavy list queries and a possible second region. — v7 PKs (ordered, uncoordinated, cursor-friendly), natural identities as UNIQUE constraints, per-tenant human numbers allocated transactionally, prefixed ids at the API.
5. *Explain.* Why is a natural key a poor primary key even though it's unique? — PKs must be immutable and meaningless; emails, slugs and numbers all change under business pressure, and every FK amplifies the change. Constrain them UNIQUE instead.
6. *Explain.* What does each key type leak, and to whom? — Identity: volume and enumerability to anyone who sees two ids. v7: creation time to anyone holding one id. v4: nothing. The leak, not aesthetics, is the reason ever to prefer v4.
7. *Explain.* Does a UUID primary key bloat secondary indexes in PostgreSQL? — No — that's InnoDB's clustered model, where secondaries store the PK. Postgres secondaries store TIDs; the real costs are the PK index itself, WAL, and 16-byte FK columns.

## Key Takeaways

- Model nouns as tables, relationships as child→parent FKs, optionality as nullability — and record every **natural identity as a UNIQUE constraint under a surrogate primary key**, because PKs must be immutable and meaningless and business identities never stay either.
- The key type is a **write-path capacity decision**: measured, v4 cost +76% index size, half-fragmented leaves, ~2× build time and ~3× steady-state WAL versus ordered keys — a bill that grows with the index and lands on replicas and backups too.
- **UUIDv7 is the modern default**: globally unique like v4, ordered like a sequence, readable timestamp, native in PostgreSQL 18 — and upgrading from v4 is a `DEFAULT` change, not a migration.
- `ORDER BY id` means newest-first only when the key is time-ordered; under v4 it's a fast, silent lie.
- Sequences gap by design; gapless numbering is a transactional feature (05.3), never the PK's job.
- Know which storage engine an argument comes from: Postgres secondaries point at TIDs, so the InnoDB everything-bloats claim doesn't transfer.
- The choice is early-or-never: every FK, index, cursor and URL inherits it. Decide with the lab's numbers on the table.

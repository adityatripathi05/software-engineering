## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

03.14 walked the shutdown from the outside: signal → stop accepting → drain → lifespan → exit, with a guillotine behind it. This notebook stands inside the process during that drain window and asks what "drain" actually consists of — because a running service is not just in-flight requests. It is supervised tasks (03.9's samplers and writers), queues holding work someone promised to do, executor threads mid-burn (04.7), shared clients and engines (04.3), and sockets owed a 1001 (03.12). Shutdown is 04.8's cancellation applied to *all of it at once* — and the discovery of this notebook is that **the order of that applying is itself a correctness property**. The same set of cancel calls, sequenced two ways, is the difference between a clean exit and a deploy that quietly deletes fifteen audit records — measured below, to the row.

### Technical Explanation

**Drain order is the data-loss policy.** A producer→queue→writer pipeline (requests enqueueing audit events, a batching writer draining them), interrupted mid-traffic:

```text
naive cancel  : produced= 28  written= 13  LOST=15
ordered drain : produced= 30  written= 30  LOST=0
```

The naive version cancels everything at once — and the queue's contents die with the writer. The ordered version is three phases with a *reason* for each boundary: **stop intake first** (cancel producers — mirroring, inside the process, what 03.14's LB-deregistration does outside it), **flush owned work** (`await queue.join()` — the writer empties what was already accepted), **then cancel** the now-idle writer. Nothing about the components changed; only the sequence did. The general rule: *work already accepted is a debt; intake is a tap.* Close the tap, pay the debt, then dismiss the workers.

**Teardown mirrors startup because dependencies point backwards.** 03.9 stated the rule; this measures the why. A flusher task whose cancellation path sends one final batch through the shared client:

```text
close client, then cancel task : final flush FAILED (client is closed)
cancel task, then close client : final flush delivered
```

Startup built client-then-task; the task therefore *depends on* the client — including in its `except CancelledError` cleanup, the dying breath 04.8 taught you to write. Teardown in startup order strands every such last duty on closed resources. Reverse order — tasks, then queues, then clients, then engine — is not tidiness; it is the dependency graph, walked leaf-first.

**The drain must escalate, and it must name names.** Cancel, wait a bounded grace, then report who refused:

```text
drained politely: ['loop_lag_sampler']
REFUSED cancellation after grace: ['legacy_poller']
```

04.8's cancel-swallower is a curiosity in a lab and a hostage-taker at shutdown: without this report, a hung drain is 03.14's silent grace-period expiry — SIGKILL, no evidence, and the next engineer debugs a "slow deploy" instead of a named task. The escalation log line converts an unbounded mystery into a one-line diagnosis.

**Some exits cannot be hurried — budget for them.** A fire-and-forgotten executor thread still working when the coroutine finishes:

```text
coroutine finished at ~0.1s; asyncio.run returned after 1.2s
```

`asyncio.run` (and uvicorn's loop shutdown) waits for the default executor's threads — every 04.8 zombie is *borrowed drain deadline*, spent inside 03.14's grace budget whether you planned it or not. Process pools have their own line item (`shutdown(cancel_futures=True)` drops the queue but running work finishes; 04.7). The deadline ladder gains its final term: **request drain + task grace + executor wait + teardown < graceful timeout < grace period** — and the executor term is the one nobody puts in the budget until a deploy times out "for no reason".

### Mental Model

> Shutdown is closing a restaurant: lock the door (intake) *before* the kitchen stops, cook what's ordered (flush the queues), send staff home in reverse hiring order (the dishwasher your chef's cleanup depends on leaves last), and write down who wouldn't leave when the lights flicked (escalation) — because the landlord (03.14's guillotine) arrives on a timer whether you're done or not. Every queue in the building holds *promises made*; drain order decides which promises a deploy silently breaks.

## How It Works

Ledgerly's drain, end to end — 03.14's outer sequence expanded into this notebook's inner one:

```text
SIGTERM → uvicorn: stop accepting; drain in-flight HTTP (03.14, budget 25s)
        → WS: close 1001 broadcast (03.12) — sockets are intake too
        └─▶ lifespan teardown begins (03.9's finally), IN THIS ORDER:
            1. STOP INTAKE      cancel producers/pollers; flip anything that
                                enqueues to reject-or-outbox
            2. PAY DEBTS        queue.join() per owned queue, each under its
                                own timeout (a debt cap, 04.8's budgets)
            3. DISMISS          cancel remaining tasks → grace → NAME refusers
            4. UNPLUG           aclose() clients (04.3 registry) → dispose engine
            5. UNHURRIABLE      executor/pool shutdown — the pre-budgeted wait
        → exit 0, inside the ladder: Σ(1..5) + HTTP drain < 25s < 30s grace
```

Two structural notes. First, **phase boundaries are awaits with timeouts**, not hopes: `queue.join()` unbounded is a drain that a stuck writer converts into SIGKILL-with-full-queue — the worst of both. Each phase spends from the remaining teardown budget (04.8's `remaining`-arithmetic, reused). Second, **what "flush" may promise depends on the queue's contract** (03.10): an in-process queue is at-most-once *at best* — ordered drain removes the *deploy* loss, but a crash (SIGKILL, OOM) still deletes whatever is queued. Drain discipline narrows the loss window; it cannot change the contract. Work that must survive even crashes was never queue-shaped — it is outbox-shaped, and shutdown is precisely where that distinction stops being theoretical.

```text
what a queued item survives:      deploy (naive)   deploy (ordered)   crash
in-process queue                        ✗                ✓              ✗
outbox row in the request txn           ✓                ✓              ✓   (03.10)
```

## Code Example

The lifespan teardown as it now reads — 03.9's `finally`, grown up:

```python
# ledgerly/lifespan.py — the finally block (startup unchanged from 04.3/04.7)
finally:
    budget = Deadline(seconds=8.0)                  # teardown's slice of the 25s

    # 1. stop intake: producers first, always
    for name in PRODUCER_TASKS:                     # pollers, schedulers, feeds
        tasks[name].cancel()
    await asyncio.wait({tasks[n] for n in PRODUCER_TASKS}, timeout=budget.take(1.0))

    # 2. pay debts: every owned queue flushes under its own cap
    for q_name, queue in owned_queues.items():
        try:
            async with asyncio.timeout(budget.take(2.0)):
                await queue.join()
        except TimeoutError:
            log.error("drain_debt_unpaid", queue=q_name, left=queue.qsize())

    # 3. dismiss with escalation: cancel, grace, NAME the refusers
    stuck = await drain_tasks(tasks, grace=budget.take(1.5))
    if stuck:
        log.error("drain_refused_cancellation", tasks=stuck)   # the one-line
                                                               # diagnosis (04.2's
    # 4. unplug, in reverse of startup                         #  dump names lines;
    for client in reversed(list(clients.values())):            #  this names tasks)
        await client.aclose()
    await engine.dispose()

    # 5. the unhurriable: pool shutdown inside what's left
    cpu_pool.shutdown(cancel_futures=True)          # queued work dropped, running
                                                    # work finishes (04.7) — budgeted
```

The load-bearing details: `budget.take()` makes every phase's timeout a *withdrawal from one account* — the phases cannot collectively exceed the teardown slice, however each behaves; the `drain_debt_unpaid` log records exactly what a stuck flush abandoned (count and queue name — the evidence 03.14's silent SIGKILL never leaves); and clients close in *reverse* registry order for the same dependency reason section B measured.

And the incident's other half — the contract fix, because drain order alone was not enough:

```python
# ❌ the November "perf win": must-happen records on an at-most-once carrier
async def emit_audit_event(action: str, resource: str) -> None:
    await audit_queue.put(AuditEvent(...))          # batched writer INSERTs later
    # at-most-once, in-process, unrecorded (03.10) — now wearing audit clothing

# ✅ restored: the audit row rides the REQUEST's transaction (04.4's fix, kept);
#    the queue remains only for genuinely best-effort telemetry
async def emit_audit_event(session: AsyncSession, action: str, resource: str) -> None:
    session.add(AuditRow(...))                      # commits with the request or
                                                    # not at all — crash-proof by
                                                    # construction (03.10, 04.6)
```

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### What may a drain promise about queued work?

- **Flush everything (`queue.join()`, generous cap):** honours every accepted item on clean deploys. *Cost:* drain time proportional to debt — a deep queue eats the whole grace; requires the debt cap and the unpaid-debt log. *Use when:* items are cheap-ish and losing them is expensive (the audit queue, before the contract fix).
- **Flush with a small cap, log the remainder:** bounded deploys, explicit losses. *Use when:* items are best-effort telemetry — losing twenty lag samples is nothing, *knowing you lost them* is hygiene.
- **Drop immediately (cancel writer, log qsize):** fastest exit. *Use when:* the queue is purely derived state someone else can rebuild (caches being warmed, prefetches).
- **Refuse the question — outbox instead:** if losing an item on *crash* is unacceptable, no drain policy suffices; the item belonged in the request's transaction (03.10). Shutdown design cannot upgrade a delivery contract; it can only stop you from discovering the contract during an audit.

**Recommendation:** classify every owned queue by "what does one lost item cost?" — the answer picks the row, and *must-happen* always picks the outbox. Then the drain code is mechanical: the phases above, each with a withdrawal from the teardown budget, and 13.x's shutdown test (below) holding the classification honest.

## Production Scenario

**Ledgerly, Tuesday 1 December, 09:20.** The SOC2 remediation plan (from 28 October's audit-attribution incident) required a *completeness* invariant alongside 04.4's attribution one: every authenticated request in the access log must have a matching audit row. The check ships Monday; its first nightly run posts results Tuesday morning: **99.94% — with the 0.06% missing in eleven tight clusters.** Someone plots the cluster timestamps against the deploy log. Eleven for eleven.

### Symptoms

- Missing audit rows — 30 to 60 per deploy — with request ids that *do* appear in access logs, all timestamped in the ~2 s before each replica's `Application shutdown complete` (03.14's logs, doing forensic duty again).
- No errors at those moments: the writer task's cancellation was 04.8-clean (re-raised, logged `audit_writer_cancelled`), lifespan teardown completed inside the ladder. Every component behaved exactly as written.
- The pattern's *start date*: 17 November — the p99 push after 04.1's slice work, when audit INSERTs were moved off the request path into a batched queue-writer ("40 ms off invoice creation, and the writer batches 50 rows per INSERT").

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert → the invariant.** No runtime signal existed or could: the loss is silent by construction (a cancelled writer doesn't error, it just stops). The completeness check is the *only* detector this class admits — 03.13's family, now guarding compliance data. Deploy-clustered absence says: shutdown path.
2. **Read the teardown.** Lifespan's `finally` (03.9-era): cancel all supervised tasks, gather, close clients. The writer — mid-batch, queue holding up to ~60 events — is cancelled *with the producers still enqueueing* and the queue's contents evaporate with it. The lab's arithmetic in production: naive cancel, LOST = whatever was in flight.
3. **The deeper wrong turn** predates the teardown: audit rows are *must-happen* records (a compliance artefact — 28 October established the stakes), and 17 November put them on an in-process queue — at-most-once, in-process, unrecorded, 03.10's contract verbatim, re-adopted in a perf review because the words "audit" and "usage event" don't rhyme. Ordered drain fixes deploys; it cannot fix a SIGKILL (03.14's guillotine, an OOM) — the queue was the wrong carrier *regardless of drain quality*.
4. **Eliminated:** the DB (rows never arrived — nothing to lose), the deploys themselves (03.14's drain is signal-correct; the loss is inside lifespan), and request-path bugs (attribution invariant clean; the rows that exist are right).

### Root Cause

Must-happen audit records were moved onto an in-process batched queue for latency, and lifespan teardown cancelled the queue's writer concurrently with its producers — so every deploy discarded the queued tail (30–60 rows), silently and errorlessly, eleven deploys in a row, detectable only by an absence invariant comparing audit rows to access logs.

### Fix

*Mitigation (same day):* the ordered drain (stop intake → `queue.join()` under a cap → cancel) deployed into lifespan teardown — deploy-time loss to zero, measured by the next night's run over Wednesday's deploys.

*Permanent fix:* the contract restored — audit rows return to the request transaction (the Code Example; ~4 ms of the 40 "won" in November given back, the rest kept via a cheaper serialisation), because a crash must not be able to delete a compliance record. The queue and its now-correct drain remain for what they always suited: best-effort telemetry batches. The eleven gaps are reconstructed from access logs into flagged synthetic audit rows, disclosed in the SOC2 remediation notes (16.x's honesty rule).

### Prevention

- **The completeness invariant stays nightly** — the only possible detector, now proven; its Grafana panel annotates deploys so the next regression is visible as clusters within a day.
- **The incident closes into a named CI test** — shutdown as a tested code path, not a hope:

```python
# tests/lifecycle/test_drain_pays_its_debts.py
async def test_deploy_during_traffic_loses_no_accepted_audit_events(app_harness):
    """Eleven deploys silently deleted 30-60 queued audit rows each (1 Dec).
    Drain = stop intake, flush owned queues, then cancel — and must-happen
    records don't ride in-process queues at all (03.10, 04.9)."""
    async with app_harness.serving_traffic(rps=50):
        accepted = await app_harness.begin_shutdown()      # SIGTERM mid-traffic
    assert await app_harness.audit_rows() >= accepted      # every accepted event landed
    assert "drain_refused_cancellation" not in app_harness.logs()
```

- The 03.10 review rule gets its costume clause: *any* record whose loss you would have to disclose rides the outbox or the request transaction — "it's just telemetry" requires saying so in the PR, where the completeness check can be pointed at it.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...tasks are cancelled in one bulk gather? | Queue contents die with their writers (measured: 15 of 28 lost). Order the phases: intake, debts, dismissal. |
| ...teardown runs in startup order? | Every task's cancellation cleanup finds its resources already closed (measured: the final flush fails). Reverse order, always — it's the dependency graph. |
| ...a task swallows its cancel at drain? | The drain hangs toward the guillotine. Bounded grace + escalation log naming the task — then the SIGKILL at least leaves a diagnosis. |
| ...a queue's flush cannot finish in its cap? | Log the unpaid debt (queue name, qsize) and move on: a bounded, *evidenced* loss beats SIGKILL-with-full-queue and no record. |
| ...executor threads are mid-work at exit? | The loop's close waits for them (measured: +1.1 s) — inside the grace budget, or the guillotine lands mid-teardown. Budget the unhurriable term. |
| ...the process is SIGKILLed anyway? | All of this never runs (03.9/03.14 canon). Ordered drain narrows the loss window; only the outbox/transaction contract closes it. |
| ...WebSockets are open at drain? | They are intake too: 1001 broadcast at phase 1 (03.12), before their feed-queues flush. |
| ...shutdown itself has a bug? | It is the least-executed, least-tested path in the codebase — that is *why* the harness test exists. Drain code without a test is a promise made in the dark. |
| ...the loss is silent by design? | Then only an absence invariant can see it (audit rows vs access log; the 03.13 family). Every must-happen stream deserves one. |

## Common Pitfalls

**Bulk-cancelling the task dict.** One `gather(*[t.cancel()...])` reads tidy and deletes queues (measured). *Instead:* phases — intake, debts, dismissal — each a bounded await.

**Teardown in startup order.** Closing clients before the tasks whose cleanup uses them. *Instead:* reverse; the mirror rule is a dependency statement, not an aesthetic.

**Unbounded `queue.join()` in teardown.** A stuck consumer turns your flush into the guillotine's countdown. *Instead:* per-queue caps from a shared teardown budget, unpaid debts logged with sizes.

**A drain that can't say who hung it.** Grace expiry with no names = 03.14's mystery slow deploy. *Instead:* the escalation report; treat any named refuser as a P2 on the task's owner.

**Upgrading a delivery contract with drain discipline.** Ordered drain made deploys clean and left crashes lossy — the queue still can't carry must-happen records. *Instead:* classify by cost-of-one-lost-item; outbox/transaction for anything you'd have to disclose.

**Shipping shutdown code untested.** It runs least, under the most concurrency, with the least observation. *Instead:* the mid-traffic SIGTERM harness test; the completeness invariant as the production backstop.

## How to Test This

- The mid-traffic drain test above: real signal, live enqueue load, assert accepted == landed and no refusers named.
- A teardown-order test: spy resources recording close order; assert reverse-of-startup and that every task's cancellation cleanup succeeded (03.9's shutdown test, upgraded with the dependency assertion).
- A refuser drill: register a deliberate swallower behind a flag in staging; assert the escalation log names it within the grace (the drill proves the diagnosis path, 04.2-style).
- The nightly completeness invariant as the permanent, production-grade assertion — per must-happen stream.

## Interview Questions

1. *Explain.* Decompose a correct in-process drain and justify the order. — Stop intake (producers/pollers/sockets), flush owned queues under caps (accepted work is debt), cancel remaining tasks with grace + escalation, unplug resources in reverse-startup order, budget the unhurriable executor wait — all inside the process's grace ladder.
2. *Debug this.* An audit table is missing 30-60 rows in clusters exactly at deploy timestamps; no errors anywhere. — Queue-buffered writes whose writer is cancelled with items queued: ordered drain for deploys, and question the carrier — must-happen records belong in the transaction/outbox, or crashes still lose them.
3. *Explain.* Why must teardown mirror startup? — Later-started components depend on earlier ones, including in their cancellation cleanup; startup-order teardown strands every dying breath on closed resources (measured: the final flush fails).
4. *Design.* A drain policy framework for a process owning five queues. — Classify each by cost-of-lost-item (flush-all / capped-flush-and-log / drop / never-queue-it), one teardown budget with per-phase withdrawals, escalation naming refusers, and a mid-traffic SIGTERM test asserting each queue's promised behaviour.
5. *Explain.* What spends your grace period that cancellation cannot hurry? — In-flight HTTP up to the drain cap, executor threads (the loop's close waits — measured +1.1 s), process-pool running work, and any flush debt; the ladder must sum them, or the guillotine lands mid-teardown.
6. *Explain.* Why is shutdown where at-most-once "collects"? — Queues concentrate accepted-but-undone work; process end is when that concentration is either paid (flush), documented (logged drop), or silently destroyed — and crash-shutdowns always choose destruction, which is why the contract, not the drain, is the real decision.
7. *Design.* Make a hung shutdown diagnosable after the SIGKILL. — Escalation log naming refusers before the guillotine, unpaid-debt logs with queue sizes, a shutdown-phase metric (last phase reached), and 03.14's absent-evidence alerting: "no `Application shutdown complete`" is itself the page.

## Key Takeaways

- Drain order is the data-loss policy: stop intake → pay debts (`queue.join()` under caps) → dismiss with escalation → unplug in reverse — measured as the difference between LOST=15 and LOST=0.
- Teardown mirrors startup because dependencies point backwards — a task's cancellation cleanup needs its resources alive (measured: the dying flush fails the other way).
- A drain must name its refusers: bounded grace + the escalation log turns 03.14's mystery hang into a one-line diagnosis; 04.8's swallower is the usual suspect.
- Budget the unhurriable: the loop's close waits for executor threads (measured +1.1 s) — zombies and pools are line items in the grace ladder, not surprises.
- Ordered drain fixes deploys; it cannot fix crashes. The queue's delivery contract (03.10) decides what a lost process loses — must-happen records ride transactions/outbox, full stop.
- Shutdown is the least-run path handling the most concurrent state: it gets its own harness test (SIGTERM mid-traffic, assert debts paid) or it is a hope.
- Silent-by-construction losses admit only absence detectors: a completeness invariant per must-happen stream, nightly, with deploys annotated on its graph.

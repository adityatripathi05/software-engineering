## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

04.2 ended with a rule: one `AsyncClient`, created in lifespan, shared by everyone. This notebook is about what that client actually *is* — a **connection pool** with limits, a queue, and timeouts — and about the day the rule's simplest reading fails. A pool is a small stock of expensive things (open TCP/TLS connections, database sessions) that requests borrow and return. Every pool in your process lives the same life: a borrower takes a connection, *holds it for the duration of its work*, and returns it; when none are free, the next borrower **queues**; when it has queued too long, it fails. You have met this life twice already — 03.11's downloads holding DB connections, 03.6's threads — and you will meet it in every module that touches a resource. This notebook makes the mechanism explicit and puts numbers on it, because both of this week's failure modes — silent queueing and cross-dependency starvation — are configuration, not fate.

### Technical Explanation

**What you are silently running on.** Captured from the pinned stack — the configuration you get by writing `httpx.AsyncClient()` with no arguments:

```text
AsyncClient() limits : Limits(max_connections=100, max_keepalive_connections=20,
                              keepalive_expiry=5.0)
AsyncClient() timeout: Timeout(timeout=5.0)          # per phase — including pool
```

Four numbers, each a decision someone else made. **`max_connections=100`**: at most 100 concurrent borrows — across *every host this client talks to*. **`max_keepalive_connections=20`**: how many idle connections are kept warm for reuse. **`keepalive_expiry=5.0`**: idle connections are discarded after 5 s — this is the *client* side of 01.7's inequality (client keep-alive < proxy idle < server keep-alive), and httpx's small default is why you rarely hit that race from this side. **`Timeout(5.0)`** applies *per phase*: `connect` (TCP+TLS establishment), `read` (waiting for bytes), `write` (sending), and **`pool` — how long a request may wait in the borrow queue** before `PoolTimeout`.

⚠️ That last phase is the one nobody knows they configured. Under pool pressure, every request first waits *silently* for up to 5 s — added latency that belongs to no dependency and appears in no dependency's graph. Exhaustion, captured (pool of 5, pool timeout 1 s, eight concurrent calls to a 2 s dependency):

```text
OK after 2.1s   × 5          ← the pool's five borrowers
PoolTimeout after 1.0s  × 3  ← failed FAST at the queue, not after 5 silent seconds
```

**The timeout taxonomy is a diagnostic instrument.** Each phase failing names a different culprit: `ConnectTimeout` → network path or dependency down; `ReadTimeout` → dependency accepted and stalled; `PoolTimeout` → **your own client is starved — the dependency may be perfectly healthy**. And one signature is a smoking gun on sight: a span whose duration *exactly equals the configured pool timeout* spent its whole life in the queue and never touched the network.

**The database pool is the same object.** SQLAlchemy's async engine defaults: `pool_size=5`, `max_overflow=10`, `pool_timeout=30` — borrow, hold (03.5's request-long session), queue, and after 30 silent seconds the canonical error, captured:

```text
TimeoutError: QueuePool limit of size 2 overflow 1 reached, connection timed out
```

Same life, different costume. The differences worth knowing: `max_overflow` allows temporary connections above `pool_size` (opened on demand, closed on return — a burst buffer httpx doesn't have), and the 30 s default `pool_timeout` means a saturated DB pool *adds half a minute of invisible queueing* before erroring — usually long after the caller's own deadline. Multiply everything by 03.14's topology: `pool_size + max_overflow` × replicas is what PostgreSQL's `max_connections` must absorb (06.8 owns that side).

### Mental Model

> Every pool is a **loan desk with a queue**: limits say how many loans exist, hold-time says how long each is out, and the pool timeout says how long you'll stand in line before walking away. Sizing is 03.11's arithmetic (`limits ÷ hold-time ≥ demand`), and *sharing* a desk is sharing a fate — whoever holds loans longest sets everyone's queue. Configure the desk per dependency, and make walking away fast: a queue you can't see is latency you can't explain.

## How It Works

The incident's mechanism, measured before it is narrated — one shared client, pool of 5, a renderer running at 2 s and a tax endpoint running at 50 ms:

```text
B. a healthy 50ms tax dependency while the renderer runs at 2s
   ONE shared client  : tax call PoolTimeout after 1.0s
   per-dependency     : tax call OK in     71 ms
```

The anatomy of the first line:

```text
        shared AsyncClient, max_connections=5
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
sends ─▶│ conn1..conn5: ALL held by /render (2s each)   │──▶ pdf-renderer (degraded)
        │                                               │
tax  ──▶│ borrow queue: [tax] … waits … PoolTimeout     │──▶ tax-api (healthy, 50ms!)
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
        the tax call never reached the network: it died at OUR loan desk,
        and its error says "timeout" with the tax provider's name nearby

        per-dependency clients (bulkheads, 02.11):
        renderer client [5] ── saturated, ITS callers queue and fail fast
        tax client      [5] ── empty, 71ms — the healthy dependency stays healthy
```

This is 02.11's webhook lesson relocated into your own process: **any worker could pick up any delivery** becomes **any request could borrow any connection**, and a pool without a concept of *destination* converts one dependency's degradation into everyone's outage. The 04.2 rule survives intact but gains its missing clause: one client per *dependency*, each created in lifespan, each with limits and timeouts sized to that dependency alone.

Sizing, in the 03.11 form, per client: `max_connections ÷ expected hold-time ≥ peak demand`, with `max_connections` also read as *the maximum concurrency you are willing to inflict on that dependency* — a courtesy bound (02.12's fate-sharing, seen from the caller's side) as much as a self-protection. The pool timeout then encodes how long queueing is acceptable before the caller's own deadline makes the wait pointless: it must be **smaller than your remaining request budget**, or you burn the budget standing in line and fail anyway.

## Code Example

Ledgerly's outbound stack after the incident — the 03.9 lifespan grown from one client into a registry of bulkheads:

```python
# ledgerly/lifespan.py (evolving 03.9's shape)
@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI) -> AsyncIterator[dict]:
    clients = {
        # sized per dependency: limits ÷ hold-time vs peak demand, in the PR
        "tax": httpx.AsyncClient(
            base_url=settings.tax_api_url,
            limits=httpx.Limits(max_connections=20, max_keepalive_connections=10),
            timeout=httpx.Timeout(connect=1.0, read=3.0, write=3.0, pool=0.5),
        ),
        # the renderer is slow BY DESIGN (2-8s renders): fewer, longer loans,
        # and a fail-fast queue so its bad days stay in its own lane
        "renderer": httpx.AsyncClient(
            base_url=settings.renderer_url,
            limits=httpx.Limits(max_connections=8, max_keepalive_connections=4),
            timeout=httpx.Timeout(connect=1.0, read=10.0, write=5.0, pool=1.0),
        ),
    }
    try:
        yield {"clients": clients, "engine": engine}
    finally:
        for c in clients.values():
            await c.aclose()
```

Two things to look at. Every number is explicit — after this notebook, an unadorned `AsyncClient()` in review is a finding, because you now know exactly which four decisions it silently makes. And the `pool=` values differ: tax calls sit on the invoice-creation path with a tight overall budget, so they walk away from the queue at 500 ms; renderer calls are slower and more patient, but *one second* of queueing still beats five silent ones.

A `PoolTimeout` must also *say what it is* — it is not the dependency's failure, and the error contract (02.9, 03.8) should not blame the innocent:

```python
# ledgerly/errors/handlers.py (extending 03.8's mapping)
@app.exception_handler(httpx.PoolTimeout)
async def pool_starved(request: Request, exc: httpx.PoolTimeout) -> JSONResponse:
    dependency = dependency_name_for(exc)          # from the client registry
    OUTBOUND_POOL_STARVED.labels(dependency).inc()
    return problem_response(                       # 02.9 catalogue
        type="https://api.ledgerly.com/problems/upstream-capacity",
        status=503, retry_after=2,
        detail=f"temporarily out of capacity toward {dependency}",
    )                                              # 503 + Retry-After: OUR capacity, not their outage
```

> **Version note** — captured on httpx 0.28-line defaults (`max_connections=100`, `max_keepalive_connections=20`, `keepalive_expiry=5.0`, `Timeout(5.0)` per phase) and SQLAlchemy 2.x (`QueuePool` message shown; async engines use the same pool semantics via `AsyncAdaptedQueuePool`). Older httpx releases defaulted timeouts differently — pin and print, don't assume. Reproduce with `_lab/lab_04_03_pools.py`.

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### One client, per-dependency clients, or per-request

- **One shared client** (04.2's first cut): simplest; one pool of warm connections. *Failure mode:* the measured head-of-line — the slowest dependency's holds starve the fastest's borrows; per-dependency limits and timeouts are impossible. *Use when:* one dependency exists, or all calls are the same shape and priority.
- **Per-dependency clients** — bulkheads with per-lane sizing, timeouts, metrics and problem types. *Disadvantages:* a registry to manage; more connections held overall. *Use when:* more than one outbound dependency matters — **Ledgerly's choice**, and the module default from here on.
- **Per-request clients:** already banned by measurement (04.2's ~350 ms sync SSL build, plus zero connection reuse). Mentioned only because it keeps reappearing in review.

### Where should the queue live — and how big should the desk be?

When demand exceeds a dependency's capacity, the excess must go *somewhere*: a **bigger pool** (more loans) pushes the concurrency onto the dependency — helpful only if it has headroom; hostile if it is already degrading (you become 02.12's inconsiderate batch client). A **longer pool timeout** absorbs bursts as invisible latency — acceptable for offline work (03.10's workers can wait), poison on a request path with its own deadline. A **shorter pool timeout** converts saturation into fast, honest 503s + `Retry-After` — load-shedding at the loan desk, the reliability ladder's fail-fast rung, and usually right for user-facing paths. The DB pool answers differently than HTTP clients: `max_overflow` gives a burst buffer worth having, and its 30 s default `pool_timeout` is almost always wrong on a request path — a request that would wait 30 s for a *connection* should have failed its own budget long before. **Recommendation:** size from 03.11's arithmetic, keep request-path pool timeouts under the caller's budget, let workers be the patient ones — and revisit when 15.14's circuit breakers give degraded dependencies a lane that fails before the queue forms.

## Production Scenario

**Ledgerly, Tuesday 20 October, 10:40.** The PDF renderer's provider deploys something; render p99 goes from 900 ms to 8 s (their status page: "elevated processing times"). At 10:43 Ledgerly's own alert fires — but on the wrong thing: `POST /v1/invoices` (creation, which calls the **tax** provider) failing at 30%, errors mapped from *timeouts on tax calls*. The tax provider's public status: green. Their latency, probed directly from a shell: 60 ms.

### Symptoms

- Invoice creation and every other tax-dependent route failing with timeout-shaped 5xx; send-invoice (renderer path) slow, which everyone expected and ignored.
- The tax spans in traces: duration **exactly 5.000 s**, no connect/TLS/first-byte events inside — uniform, suspiciously round.
- The tax provider healthy by every external measure; on-call burns twenty minutes confirming what their graphs already said.
- Loop lag (04.1) flat; no sampler gaps (04.2); CPU normal — the process is healthy, *waiting politely*.

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert → Metrics.** Tax "timeouts" with a healthy tax provider and a healthy loop leaves the space between: the client machinery. The 04.1/04.2 signatures are absent by inspection — this is neither slices nor a freeze.
2. **Read the exception type, not the route name.** The mapped errors are `httpx.PoolTimeout` — not `ConnectTimeout`, not `ReadTimeout`. The taxonomy speaks: these requests *never reached the network*. The 5.000 s span duration equals the default pool timeout — the whole span is queue.
3. **Who holds the pool?** One shared lifespan client (04.2's fix, adopted fleet-wide three weeks ago) with default `max_connections=100`. Renderer calls now hold 8 s each; invoice sends run ~12-15 concurrent per replica... at 8 s holds that's ~100 connections camped on `/render` within a minute of the renderer degrading. Every tax borrow queues behind them and dies at 5 s.
4. **Eliminated on the way:** the tax provider (direct probes, their metrics), DNS (04.2's executor path — resolution appears in no failing span because no connection was attempted), and capacity (35 idle threads, low CPU — the starvation is inside one object).

### Root Cause

All outbound traffic shared one connection pool with no concept of destination; when the renderer's hold-time quadrupled, its calls accumulated all 100 loans, and tax requests — to a perfectly healthy dependency — queued at the shared desk until the silent 5 s pool timeout expired, surfacing as "tax timeouts" that pointed the entire diagnosis at the wrong company.

### Fix

*Mitigation (11:02):* config redeploy: renderer `read` timeout cut to 4 s (halves the hold), renderer calls capped by a semaphore at 10 per replica. The camped connections drain; tax calls stop queueing; creation recovers by 11:09 while renders stay slow (correctly — that half *is* degraded).

*Permanent fix:* the per-dependency client registry (Code Example) — bulkheads sized per dependency, `pool=` timeouts under the caller's budget, `PoolTimeout` mapped to the `upstream-capacity` problem type (503 + `Retry-After`) so the error stops blaming healthy dependencies.

### Prevention

- **Per-dependency pool metrics**: in-use / queued / limit for every client and the DB engine, with an alert at 80% sustained — pool pressure becomes visible *before* it becomes latency. The dashboard panel sits beside 03.11's DB-pool panel; same loan desk, same graph shape.
- **The incident closes into a named CI test** on the assembled app:

```python
# tests/contract/test_outbound_clients_are_bulkheaded.py
def test_every_dependency_has_its_own_sized_client(app_state) -> None:
    """One shared pool turns any dependency's bad day into every dependency's
    outage (20 Oct). Every outbound dependency gets its own client, every
    client gets explicit limits and a full four-phase timeout (04.3)."""
    for name, client in app_state["clients"].items():
        assert client._limits.max_connections is not None, f"{name}: unbounded pool"
        t = client.timeout
        assert None not in (t.connect, t.read, t.write, t.pool), \
            f"{name}: incomplete timeout — a missing phase is an infinite default"
```

- The runbook gains the taxonomy table: *connect* = them/network, *read* = them slow, *pool* = **us** — with the span-duration-equals-config tell written down, so the next 5.000 s span is diagnosed in one minute, not twenty.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...a dependency slows down 4×? | Its holds lengthen; its own pool saturates and *its* callers fail fast at the pool — nobody else notices. That containment is what the bulkhead bought. |
| ...the pool is exhausted? | Borrowers queue silently for `pool`/`pool_timeout`, then `PoolTimeout`/`TimeoutError`. Decide the timeout; never inherit 5 s (httpx) or 30 s (SQLAlchemy) on a request path. |
| ...a span's duration exactly equals a configured timeout? | It spent its life in a queue or wait, never doing work — read which phase, and you have the culprit's address. |
| ...the DB pool and the HTTP pools compete? | They don't share connections but do share request budgets: a request holding a DB connection (03.5) while queueing at an HTTP pool multiplies hold-times across desks — 03.11's arithmetic, now with two loans per request. |
| ...traffic ×10? | Demand exceeds `limits ÷ hold-time`: queues form at the smallest desk first. The 80% alert names it; shedding (fast 503 + Retry-After) beats queueing. |
| ...keepalive idles past the server's window? | 01.7 from the client seat: `keepalive_expiry` (5 s default) must stay below every intermediary's idle timeout, or reused connections meet closing sockets. |
| ...a connection in the pool has silently died? | HTTP clients notice on use and retry the connect; DB pools need `pool_pre_ping`/`pool_recycle` (06.8) — a stale loan is worse than a slow one. |
| ...the same request needs the same dependency twice? | Two borrows, possibly interleaved with other holds — deadlock-shaped under tiny pools. Keep per-request borrows short and sequential; never hold across an unrelated await (03.11's rule generalised). |

## Common Pitfalls

**One client for all dependencies.** The incident: shared pool, shared fate, and errors that blame whoever the starved caller *wanted* to reach. *Instead:* a client per dependency, in lifespan, in a registry the tests can walk.

**Trusting default limits and timeouts.** `AsyncClient()` means 100 shared connections and 5 silent seconds of queueing; a bare engine means 30. *Instead:* every number explicit; unadorned constructors are review findings.

**Reading `PoolTimeout` as the dependency's failure.** It is your capacity, not their outage — mislabeling it cost twenty minutes and nearly a wrong escalation. *Instead:* map it to its own problem type (503, `Retry-After`), count it per dependency.

**Raising limits as the reflex fix.** A bigger desk pushes more concurrency onto a dependency that may already be drowning, and defers the queue one bad day. *Instead:* size from hold-time and demand; fix hold-times first (timeouts), shed honestly.

**Absorbing saturation as queue time.** A long pool timeout converts overload into unexplained latency — the worst observability trade available. *Instead:* fail fast on request paths; let 03.10's workers be the patient ones.

**Holding one pool's loan while queueing at another.** DB connection held across an HTTP borrow (or vice versa) — hold-times multiply and deadlocks lurk at small sizes. *Instead:* borrow late, return early, never across unrelated awaits.

## Key Takeaways

- Every pool is the same object — borrow, hold, queue, timeout — whether it hands out HTTP connections, DB sessions, or threads; learn it once, operate it everywhere.
- The defaults are decisions: httpx = 100 shared connections + 5 s per phase *including a silent queue*; SQLAlchemy = 5+10 with 30 s. Print them, override them, test that they're explicit.
- `PoolTimeout` means *us*, not *them* — give it its own problem type and metric, and read span-duration == configured-timeout as "the whole span was queue".
- One client per dependency is 02.11's bulkhead inside your process: the measured difference between a 1 s death at a starved desk and 71 ms to a healthy dependency.
- Size by 03.11's arithmetic (`limits ÷ hold-time ≥ demand`), bound by courtesy to the dependency, and keep request-path pool timeouts under the caller's own budget.
- When demand exceeds capacity, choose where the excess goes on purpose: bigger desk (pressure them), longer queue (hide it as latency), or fast shed (503 + Retry-After) — request paths shed, workers wait.
- Pool pressure metrics (in-use/queued/limit, 80% alert) turn this whole class into a dashboard line that moves *before* the incident.

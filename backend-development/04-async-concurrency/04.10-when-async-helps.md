> ⚡ **Wednesday 9 December, 08:55** — finance's pre-close report contains duplicated summary rows, and the reporting tool's logs are full of a database error nobody had seen before November's "modernisation". The cause: `gather()` wrapped around a workload whose concurrency was 1 all along.

## Concept

### Plain-English Explanation

Nine notebooks have taught the machinery; this one asks the only question that was ever commercial: *what is async actually for, and when is it worth its price?* The product is precise: **holding many waits at once, cheaply**. That is the entire offer. Async does not make code faster, does not make the database faster, does not add CPU, and does not make a sequential job parallel. It makes *waiting* nearly free — so a workload that consists of many overlapping waits can be served by one small process instead of an army of threads. Whether that offer is worth taking depends on two questions about the workload, not one question about fashion: **does it wait?** and **does it wait concurrently?** Two yeses and async is the right tool. Anything else, and you are paying this module's tax — the eight incidents behind us — for a product you aren't using.

### Technical Explanation

**The product, measured.** 200 concurrent requests against a 100 ms dependency, one uvicorn worker, async handler vs `def` handler — with the *server* reporting its own concurrency (the honest metric; client-side request overhead on a single lab machine drowns wall-clock comparisons, so we ask the server how many waits it actually held):

```text
endpoint      wall   max in-flight (server's view)
/io-async    8.61s   200 concurrent waits held
/io-def      9.46s    40 concurrent waits held        ← the thread-seat ceiling (03.6)
```

The async worker parked all 200 waits at once; the `def` worker is structurally capped at its 40 thread seats — 5 waves through the dependency, and every wave of queueing lands in p95. And the waits really are nearly free:

```text
10,000 sleeping tasks created, held and completed in 0.43s total
```

A parked task is a heap object — no stack reservation, no kernel thread, no scheduler pressure. Ten thousand concurrent waits cost less than half a second *in total*; ten thousand threads is not a number threads do. This asymmetry is the entire economic case, and 03.11/03.12's connection-heavy workloads (sockets, streams, long polls) are its natural customers.

**The non-product, measured.** The same 200 requests with 30 ms of CPU each instead of the wait:

```text
/cpu-async  11.94s   (serialised on the loop, 04.1's law)
/cpu-def     9.88s   (limping through the GIL, 04.7's row two)
```

Six seconds of total burning on one core comes out as ~10 s of wall either way — **nobody wins**, because there is no waiting to overlap. Async cannot buy throughput for work that never waits; that was 04.7's matrix, now shown at the architecture level.

**The third failure of fit — concurrency the workload doesn't have.** The incident's mechanism, miniaturised: twenty inserts pushed through `gather()` over one shared connection (asyncpg's one-operation-at-a-time law), with the retry-until-done handling real code grows:

```text
gather + retries over 1 connection: 0.62s, 210 attempts, 190 'op in progress' errors
plain sequential loop             : 0.62s,  20 attempts,   0 errors
```

Identical wall time — the connection serialises the work regardless of what the syntax promises — and the `gather` bought only errors and retry churn. ⚠️ Syntax cannot add concurrency that the *resources* don't permit and the *correctness* doesn't allow: one connection, one sequence-dependent report, one file handle — these have concurrency 1, and dressing them in `gather` adds every 04.5-class hazard while the bottleneck yawns.

**The tax table.** The price of the product is this module's own ledger: slices tax everyone (04.1), one sync call freezes everything (04.2), every await is a race seam (04.5), context needs new plumbing (04.4), cancellation has teeth (04.8), and shutdown becomes an ordering discipline (04.9). None of these exist — or they exist in milder, better-understood forms — in a boring threaded service. The tax is real, permanent, and paid in review vigilance and incident hours. The only question is whether the concurrent-waiting product is worth it *for this service's workload*.

### Mental Model

> Async is **bulk-buying warehouse space for waiting**. If your workload is a thousand parcels all in transit at once, the warehouse is the whole business — nothing else stores waiting that cheaply. If your parcels arrive one at a time (sequential), or never sit (CPU), you are paying rent — the concurrency-bug tax, this module's eight incidents — on empty shelves. Rent is charged on the *model*, but revenue only comes from the *workload*. Audit the workload first.

## How It Works

The decision, as the flow every "should this be async?" review walks:

```text
does the workload WAIT (I/O-dominated)?
 ├─ no (CPU-dominated) ──────────────▶ processes/workers own it (04.7);
 │                                     the web tier in front can be either
 └─ yes: do many waits OVERLAP?
     ├─ no (sequential pipeline,     ▶ SYNC — a plain loop; async adds
     │   batch job, CLI, one conn)     hazards to a concurrency of 1 (measured: D)
     └─ yes: how many at once?
         ├─ ≤ the thread-seat budget ▶ EITHER works; choose by ecosystem &
         │   (~40-100, 03.6)           team — sync is cheaper to hold correctly
         └─ ≫ thread seats          ▶ ASYNC earns its rent (measured: 200 vs 40)
             (fan-out, sockets,       — and you staff for the tax
              streams, long polls)

legitimate hybrids (not compromises):
  async app + def endpoints (03.6)    sync-native routes ride the threadpool
  async web tier + sync workers       the 02.10/03.10 split most systems want
  async gateway + sync internals      concurrency at the edge, simplicity behind
```

Two clarifications the flow chart compresses. **"How many at once" is measurable before committing**: peak concurrent in-flight requests (the 04.3 pool metrics), concurrent open sockets (03.12's budget), fan-out width per request — numbers your existing telemetry already holds. And **the hybrid rows are first-class**: Ledgerly itself is the first one — an async app where sync-native code runs as `def` endpoints on the threadpool — and most real estates are the second: async where the waiting concentrates (the API tier), sync where work is sequential anyway (workers chewing a queue one job at a time).

## Code Example

The incident's before and after — the whole diff that mattered:

```python
# ❌ November's "modernisation" of the summary builder (ledgerly-reports)
async def build_summaries(session: AsyncSession, period: str) -> None:
    customers = await fetch_customers(session, period)
    await asyncio.gather(*(                      # "async = fast", the PR said
        build_customer_summary(session, c, period) for c in customers
    ))                                           # ⚠️ one session, one connection:
                                                 # gather adds contention, not speed

# ✅ December's fix: the workload's true shape, written honestly
async def build_summaries(session: AsyncSession, period: str) -> None:
    for customer in await fetch_customers(session, period):
        await build_customer_summary(session, customer, period)
    # same wall time (measured), zero 'operation in progress' errors,
    # and the sequence the summary maths silently relied on is explicit again
```

If the job ever genuinely needs parallelism, the honest version is resource-shaped, not syntax-shaped — N *sessions* from the pool (04.3), bounded by a semaphore (04.5), each task owning its connection:

```python
# the parallel version, IF measurement ever justifies it: concurrency follows
# the resources — one session PER task, capped below the pool size
async def build_summaries_parallel(sessionmaker, period: str, width: int = 4) -> None:
    cap = asyncio.Semaphore(width)                     # width < pool_size (04.3)

    async def one(customer: CustomerId) -> None:
        async with cap, sessionmaker() as session:     # own connection, own txn
            await build_customer_summary(session, customer, period)
            await session.commit()                     # 04.6: each summary atomic

    async with asyncio.TaskGroup() as tg:
        for customer in await fetch_customers_ids(period):
            tg.create_task(one(customer))
```

The contrast carries the notebook's thesis: the second version is *more* code and *more* moving parts — justified only when the measured bottleneck is genuinely the per-customer wait and the database has headroom for `width` concurrent transactions. Concurrency is an engineering purchase with a receipt, never a syntax default.

> **Version note** — the lab's head-to-head runs client and server on one machine, so wall-clock comparisons carry client-side request overhead; the server-reported **max in-flight** gauge is the load-bearing number (200 vs 40), and the one to reproduce: `_lab/lab_04_10_when_async.py`. The one-connection law in section D is asyncpg's real behaviour (`another operation is in progress`), miniaturised for determinism.

## Design Patterns / Tradeoffs

### Choosing a concurrency model per service

- **Async end-to-end** (Ledgerly's API tier): *for* high overlapping-wait concurrency — fan-out to dependencies, sockets, streams. *Costs:* the full module-04 tax, staffed and tooled (sampler, strict-loop CI, import guards — the tax is manageable *because* those exist). *Wrong when* adopted as identity: every internal tool inheriting the hazard class for workloads of ten concurrent requests.
- **Sync + threads** (a classic WSGI service): *for* workloads inside the thread-seat budget; the bug classes are older, better documented, and the ecosystem never needs auditing for hidden sync calls. *Costs:* the hard ceiling (measured: 40), per-thread memory, and no cheap answer to sockets/streams. *Wrong when* concurrency is the product — chat, feeds, long polls.
- **The hybrids** — async app + `def` routes (03.6); async edge + sync workers (02.10/03.10's split); sequential-async (the fixed report builder: async syntax for ecosystem fit, concurrency of 1 by design). All first-class; the last one deserves naming because it is common and correct — *using* async libraries does not oblige you to *manufacture* concurrency.

**Recommendation:** decide per service from measured workload shape (peak overlap, wait/burn ratio); default new *API-tier* services async (Ledgerly's tooling amortises the tax), default internal/batch tools sync or sequential-async; and require any `gather`/`TaskGroup` in review to name its resource budget (whose connections? capped by what?) — parallelism requests are pool-sizing requests wearing syntax.

## Production Scenario

**Ledgerly, Wednesday 9 December, 08:55.** Finance runs the December pre-close report from `ledgerly-reports` — an internal batch tool that summarises each customer's period activity. The output has **duplicated summary rows** for eleven customers; the pre-close checklist (which reconciles totals, 03.13's habit spreading) catches the doubled totals before they reach anyone's books.

### Symptoms

- Duplicate summary rows — same customer, same period, differing timestamps — for a scattered subset of customers; totals exactly 2× for those rows.
- The tool's logs since mid-November: intermittent `asyncpg.exceptions._base.InterfaceError: cannot perform operation: another operation is in progress`, retried at the job level by the runner's catch-all ("transient DB error → rerun batch"), which is where the duplicates were born: reruns re-inserted customers the failed pass had already committed.
- Timeline: the errors begin exactly at the November "modernisation" release — a rewrite of the tool from its old synchronous loop to async, PR description: *"async rewrite for performance"*. Runtime before: 41 minutes. Runtime after: **41 minutes.**

### Diagnosis

1. **Alert → the reconciliation.** Wrong-data class again (03.13's family): no crash, exit code 0, duplicates found only by an invariant. The job-level retry is the duplicator; the InterfaceError is the trigger; the rewrite is the origin — three layers, walked backward.
2. **Read the error's law.** asyncpg: one operation at a time per connection. The rewrite wrapped per-customer builders in `gather()` — dozens of coroutines sharing *one* `AsyncSession`, hence one connection. Every overlapping pair beyond the first raises; the lab's miniature reproduces it 190 times in 20 inserts.
3. **Measure the counterfactual** (the lab's D): sequential and gather-with-retries complete in the *same wall time* — the single connection serialises the work no matter what the syntax claims. The rewrite's performance gain was structurally zero before its first bug; the 41-minute runtime never changing was the tell nobody read.
4. **The retry made it data-corrupting:** batch-level rerun without idempotency (02.8's lesson, internal edition) re-ran customers whose INSERTs had committed in the failed pass. Partial success + coarse retry = duplicates — 04.6's double-write, self-inflicted.

### Root Cause

A sequential batch workload — one connection, order-dependent maths, concurrency 1 by nature — was rewritten around `gather()` on the belief that async is a performance upgrade; the shared connection made the added concurrency purely destructive (interface errors → coarse retries → duplicate inserts) while buying zero speed, because the workload's bottleneck was never overlapping waits.

### Fix

*Mitigation (same morning):* the eleven duplicate rows deleted by the reconciliation's own report; the job rerun with `gather` hot-patched to a loop; clean output by 10:20.

*Permanent fix:* the sequential builder restored *as the design* (Code Example), with the parallel variant documented behind a measurement gate — session-per-task, semaphore below the pool size, per-customer transactions, and an idempotent `(customer, period)` unique constraint (04.6's arbiter) so any future rerun is safe regardless. The runner's catch-all retry replaced with per-customer retry under that constraint.

### Prevention

- **The incident closes into a named CI test** — the law the rewrite broke, pinned where reports' code lives:

```python
# tests/reports/test_no_shared_session_across_tasks.py
async def test_summary_builder_never_shares_a_session_across_tasks(monkeypatched_session):
    """gather() over one AsyncSession bought zero speed and eleven duplicated
    summaries (9 Dec). A session is single-flight property: concurrent use is
    a bug at ANY speed (04.10, 04.3)."""
    with concurrent_use_detector(monkeypatched_session) as detector:
        await build_summaries(monkeypatched_session, period="2026-11")
    assert detector.max_concurrent_operations == 1
```

- The review rule from Tradeoffs, adopted: any new `gather`/`TaskGroup` names its resource budget in the PR (whose connections, capped how) — no budget named, no parallelism merged.
- The rewrite checklist gains the tell: **a performance rewrite that doesn't change the measured runtime has not changed the bottleneck** — 41 minutes before, 41 after, was the whole review in one number.

## Failure-First Checklist

| What if... | Answer |
|---|---|
| ...the workload is many overlapping waits? | Async's home turf: 200 held waits vs 40 thread seats, measured. Pay the tax, run the tooling (sampler, strict loop, guards). |
| ...the workload is CPU? | Neither model wins (measured: ~10 s both ways); 04.7 owns it — processes, workers, bounded inputs. |
| ...the workload is sequential? | Concurrency 1: a loop. Async *syntax* is fine (ecosystem fit); manufactured concurrency adds 04.5's hazards to nothing (measured: same wall, 190 errors). |
| ...`gather` wraps a shared resource? | The resource's law wins: one connection/session/file serialises regardless; the syntax only adds contention errors. Parallelism = resource-per-task + a cap. |
| ...a "performance rewrite" changes no runtime? | The bottleneck didn't move — the rewrite bought risk, not speed. The unchanged number is the review. |
| ...concurrency needs outgrow thread seats mid-life? | The hybrid ladder: `def`→async per route (03.6), or async at the edge only — migration by workload, not by campaign. |
| ...the team hasn't operated async before? | The tax arrives before the revenue: budget the tooling (04.1's sampler, 04.2's guards, CI fixtures) as part of adoption, or choose sync inside the seat budget. |
| ...retries wrap a partially-succeeding batch? | 04.6's double-write via 02.8's missing idempotency — the constraint and per-item retry, whatever the concurrency model. |

## Common Pitfalls

**Async as a performance adjective.** It is a concurrency *model* for waiting; where nothing waits concurrently, it accelerates nothing (measured twice: CPU and sequential shapes). *Instead:* name the waits and their overlap before the rewrite PR exists.

**`gather` as a "make faster" wrapper.** Concurrency follows resources: one session in, contention out. *Instead:* resource-per-task, semaphore-capped, or admit the loop.

**Model by identity.** "We're an async shop" applied to a 10-RPS internal tool buys the full hazard class for empty shelves. *Instead:* per-service judgement from workload shape; hybrids are respectable.

**Ignoring the tell of unchanged runtime.** 41 minutes → 41 minutes shipped anyway. *Instead:* rewrites carry before/after measurements or they are refactors, reviewed as risk-only changes.

**Counting the product, forgetting the tax.** The module's ledger — slices, freezes, races, drain losses — is the recurring cost of the model. *Instead:* adopt the tooling with the model; the tax is cheap only when instrumented.

**Batch retries without idempotency.** The duplicator in this incident and every one like it. *Instead:* unique constraints per unit of work (04.6); retry the unit, not the batch.

## Interview Questions

1. *Explain.* What does async actually buy, and what does it cost? — Cheap concurrent waiting (10k parked tasks in 0.43 s; 200 held waits vs 40 thread seats, measured) at the price of a permanent hazard class: slice budgets, blocking audits, await-point races, cancellation and drain discipline.
2. *Debug this.* A batch tool rewritten async shows intermittent "another operation is in progress", unchanged runtime, and duplicated output rows after retries. — `gather` over one connection/session: serialised anyway, erroring under overlap, duplicated by coarse retries. Sequentialise (or session-per-task + cap), add per-unit idempotency.
3. *Explain.* Why does async not help CPU-bound services? — No waits to overlap: the loop serialises burns, threads limp through the GIL (both measured ~equal); 04.7's processes/workers own computation.
4. *Design.* Choose concurrency models for: a public API fanning out to 5 dependencies; a nightly ETL; a websocket presence service; an internal CRUD admin at 5 RPS. — Async (overlapping waits at scale); sequential/sync (concurrency 1, simplicity wins); async (thousands of parked sockets is the product); either — so sync/def, cheapest to hold correctly.
5. *Explain.* When is `gather` an anti-pattern even in a correctly-async service? — When the fanned-out work shares a single-flight resource or a correctness-ordering: the resource serialises, the syntax adds races and contention. Parallelism is granted by resources and budgets, not by the keyword.
6. *Design.* An adoption plan for async in a team new to it. — Workload audit first (overlap numbers); pilot on the genuinely concurrent service; land the tooling with it (lag sampler + alert, strict-loop CI, sync-import guards, drain tests); hybrids for the rest; revisit with measurements, not sentiment.
7. *Explain.* Your rewrite's runtime didn't change. What does that prove? — The bottleneck was never what the rewrite changed: the work is resource- or sequence-bound. The rewrite delivered risk at zero yield — measure the bottleneck before touching the model.

## Key Takeaways

- Async sells one product: bulk cheap waiting — 200 concurrent waits held vs 40 thread seats, 10,000 parked tasks in 0.43 s. Everything else it "sells" is a misunderstanding.
- Two questions decide fit: does it wait, and do the waits overlap? CPU shapes (nobody wins, measured) go to 04.7; sequential shapes (zero speedup, 190 errors, measured) stay loops.
- Concurrency is granted by resources, not syntax: `gather` over one session buys contention at identical wall time — parallelism means resource-per-task under a cap (04.3, 04.5).
- The tax is this module's ledger — slices, freezes, races, context, cancellation, drain — permanent, and affordable exactly insofar as the tooling (sampler, guards, fixtures) ships with the model.
- Hybrids are first-class: async app + `def` routes, async edge + sync workers, sequential-async batch code. Model per service, by measured workload shape — never by identity.
- An unchanged runtime after a performance rewrite is a complete review in one number: the bottleneck didn't move; the risk did.
- Batch retries without per-unit idempotency turn any transient error into duplicate data (04.6 + 02.8) — under every concurrency model ever.
